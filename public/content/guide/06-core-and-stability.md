# Core & Stability

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - Your core's main job is to **stop unwanted movement**: resisting bending backwards (anti-extension), twisting (anti-rotation) and side-bending (anti-lateral-flexion) while your legs and arms do the work, and passing force between them.
> - **Breathing and bracing** come first: a moderate 360° brace and a steady exhale on the effort. No maximal breath-hold straining (Valsalva) before full clearance.
> - **Right now your core training is arm-free:** dead bugs, hollow holds, 90/90 breathing, single-leg work and balance next to a support. Hip and ankle stability is core work for uneven ground.
> - **Later**, by gate: Pallof presses and woodchops once grip is cleared; planks, side planks and bird dogs (McGill-style) only at full clearance, because they bear weight through the wrist.
> - Core work grows into **loaded carries**: suitcase, then farmer's, then sandbag carries. That's where core stability and farmer strength meet.

Core stability on a hike means something specific. Your trunk stays steady under a pack while one foot lands on a rock and the other swings past. You keep your balance when the ground tilts. You carry a heavy bag up a staircase without leaning. This chapter shows how to build that, starting with what you can do safely today.

---

## What your core is for

Think of your trunk as the link between your legs and your arms. When it's stiff in the right way at the right moment, force passes through cleanly and your spine isn't forced into positions it dislikes. When it's floppy, energy leaks and the lower back or hips take the strain.

For you, that means:
- **Hiking with a pack on uneven ground:** keeping the pelvis level on one leg, stopping the pack twisting you, and controlling descents.
- **Carrying like a farmer:** standing tall with a heavy load in one or both hands without collapsing sideways.
- **Getting up and down:** off the floor, out of a tent, over a stile, with control.
- **Protecting the healing arm:** a stable trunk and steady balance mean fewer stumbles, and stumbles are your biggest risk right now.

## The jobs of the core

| Job | What it means | Real-life example | Now (arm-free) | Later (gate) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anti-extension | Stop the lower back arching | Pack pulling backwards, reaching overhead | Dead bug (legs only), tucked hollow hold | Plank, hanging knee raise (Full clearance) |
| Anti-rotation | Stop the trunk twisting | Stepping over rocks, one-sided loads | Single-leg RDL, split squats, step-ups (the pelvis must not twist) | Pallof press, woodchop (Grip cleared) |
| Anti-lateral-flexion | Stop side-bending | Suitcase in one hand, uneven ground | Standing tall on one leg (side-lying hip abduction, single-leg balance) | Suitcase carry (Grip cleared, the 70% rung with your physio's OK), then side plank and Copenhagen (Full clearance) |
| Hip and pelvic control | Keep the pelvis level on one leg | Every stride on a slope | Single-leg balance, side-lying hip abduction, the adductor-squeeze bridge (lateral step-ups from Phase 2a) | Loaded single-leg work, hops on uneven ground |
| Producing rotation | Turn powerfully through hips and upper back | Throwing, swinging an axe, scrambling | — | Woodchops (Grip cleared), med-ball slams, get-ups, sandbag work (Full clearance) |
| Breathing and pressure | Brace without holding your breath | Every heavy lift and steep climb | 90/90 breathing, exhaling on the effort | The same, under heavier loads |

## Breathing and bracing

### Why breathing comes first

Your diaphragm is both a breathing muscle and part of your core. In a lab study, spine stability was higher at greater lung volumes with active back muscles. It fell when the lungs were low and the trunk muscles were quiet, and one participant whose breathing and trunk muscles didn't coordinate well had compromised stability ([Wang & McGill, 2008](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22McGill%20SM%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22core%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22endurance%20times%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22stability%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=10)) **[Moderate evidence]**. The skill you want is to **breathe behind a brace**: keep the trunk firm while you keep breathing.

**90/90 breathing** (in your daily mobility, 5 slow breaths):
1. Lie on your back with your calves on a bench, hips and knees at about 90°. Get down using a bench and your uninjured hand, never your injured arm.
2. Breathe in through your nose for about 4 seconds, letting your belly, sides and lower back expand.
3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for about 6 seconds, until your ribs drop and your lower back feels heavy on the floor.
4. Pause for a second, then repeat.

### How to brace (moderately)

Bracing means stiffening the whole trunk, front, sides and back, rather than sucking the belly in. In a modelling study, a full abdominal brace improved lumbar stability by about 32% at the cost of about 15% more compression, while "hollowing" (drawing the belly button in) added very little ([Grenier & McGill, 2007](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22McGill%20SM%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22core%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22endurance%20times%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22stability%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=10)) **[Moderate evidence]**.

1. Stand tall, ribs stacked over your pelvis.
2. Take a normal breath in, not a huge one.
3. Tighten your trunk as if someone were about to poke you in the belly. For most exercises, about a third of your maximum is enough **[Expert opinion]**.
4. Keep breathing, short and steady, while you hold the stiffness. On the hard part of each rep, **breathe out** through pursed lips.

### Breath-holding (Valsalva): not yet

A Valsalva manoeuvre is holding your breath and straining against a closed throat. It raises intra-abdominal pressure and goes with a jump in blood pressure during heavy lifting, though a review concluded its health risks during resistance exercise "remain unconfirmed" ([Hackett & Chow, 2013](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22valsalva%20maneuver%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22resistance%20exercise%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. While your arm heals the rule is simple: **moderate bracing and exhale on effort, no maximal breath-holds**; your loads are chosen so you never need one. How a full brace works on heavy sets after clearance is in [Strength Technique](05-strength-technique.md).

> [!SAFETY]
> Dizziness, a headache that comes on while lifting, or seeing stars means you're straining too hard or holding your breath. Stop, sit down, and breathe normally. A sudden, severe headache needs urgent medical help (999).

## Your arm-free core, now (Phases 0–1)

All of this is in your sessions already, and all of it is done **without your injured arm** taking any weight, gripping or bracing.

| Exercise | Where in your plan | Dose | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dead bug, legs only | Strength A | 3 × 6–8 per side, slow | Anti-extension: the lower back stays heavy on the mat while a leg reaches away |
| Tucked hollow hold | Strength B | 3 × 30 s, building to 40 s | Anti-extension under tension; breathing behind the brace |
| 90/90 breathing | Daily mobility | 5 slow breaths | Breathing control, rib position |
| Single-leg RDL | Strength B | 3 × 8–12 per side | Anti-rotation: the hips stay square as you hinge on one leg |
| Single-leg hip thrust | Strength B | 3 × 10–15 per side | Anti-rotation: the hips stay level while one leg drives |
| Rear-foot-elevated split squats and Patrick steps; step-ups in the circuit | Strength A and C | 2–3 × 10–15 per side | Pelvic control on one leg |
| Side-lying hip abduction | Strength B | 2 × 15–20 per side | Side-hip strength that keeps the pelvis level on slopes |
| Adductor-squeeze bridge | Strength C | 3 × 12–15, 3-s squeeze | Inner-thigh strength that steadies the knee on side-slopes |
| Single-leg balance, eyes open | Daily mobility (every day) | 30 s per side | Ankle and hip control, next to a support |

That's deliberately short: each Phase 0–1 session has at most six exercises, so each one gets your full effort. Planks and extra floor work come later. The **hollow-hold test** on Progress → Tests tracks your trunk endurance every 4 weeks, done on the mat straight after the warm-up's 90/90 breathing.

### Getting down to the floor and up again, arm-free

The method lives in [Mobility & Movement Quality](07-mobility.md#getting-to-and-from-the-floor): a bench on your uninjured side, your uninjured hand only, your injured arm across your stomach, and a roll onto your uninjured side to get up. To keep the number of trips down, every strength session batches its floor work into one visit: the dead bug ends Strength A, and Strength B finishes with side-lying hip work, single-leg hip thrusts and the hollow hold back to back. If any step ever needs your injured arm, use a higher surface, such as a bench or treatment couch, instead of the floor.

## Stability for uneven ground: ankles and hips

The terrain goal (hiking anywhere) is mostly a **stability** problem. It's about how well your ankles and hips catch small, unexpected tilts.

**Balance training works.** Pooled trials in athletes found that balance and proprioceptive training cut ankle sprains by about 38–46% ([de Vasconcelos et al., 2018; Bellows & Wong, 2018](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22balance%20training%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22proprioceptive%22%29%20AND%20TITLE:%22ankle%20sprain%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22meta-analysis%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22systematic%20review%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22randomised%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22randomized%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=6)) **[Strong evidence]**. An unsupervised home programme cut repeat sprains from 33% to 22% ([Hupperets et al., 2009](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22balance%20training%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22proprioceptive%22%29%20AND%20TITLE:%22ankle%20sprain%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22meta-analysis%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22systematic%20review%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22randomised%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22randomized%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=6)) **[Strong evidence]**. Even if you've never sprained an ankle, the mechanism is the same: better reflexes and control at the ankle.

**Hips steer the knees.** In a landing study, greater hip rotational stiffness went with less knee collapse inwards during single-leg landings ([Cannon et al., 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22McGill%20SM%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22core%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22endurance%20times%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22stability%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=10)) **[Moderate evidence]**. That's why your plan includes side-lying hip abduction, the adductor-squeeze bridge and single-leg work from day one, with lateral step-ups in the circuits from Phase 2a.

**Your balance progression.** Always next to a support your uninjured hand can touch. Eyes-closed work waits for full clearance, because with your eyes shut a stumble is far more likely.

| Stage | What to do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Single-leg stand, eyes open, 30 s | 30 s steady on both sides |
| 2 | Add slow head turns left and right | 30 s without touching the support |
| 3 | Free-leg reaches: tap forwards, sideways and back | 3 slow reaches each way, no wobble |
| 4 (Full clearance) | Short eyes-closed spells (5–10 s), fingertips on the support | 3 × 10 s each side |
| 5 (Full clearance) | Unsupported work, then uneven surfaces outdoors, then small hops and landings (from Phase 4) | — |

> [!WARNING]
> No wobble boards, foam pads, BOSU-type domes or balance cushions until full clearance. They add fall risk without adding much that hiking needs. Do your balance work on a firm floor and let the trail be the "unstable surface" later.

**Ankle strength and mobility** matter just as much:
- **Tibialis raises** (Strength A, and in later circuits): the shin muscles that lift your toes. They stop you catching a toe and control the foot on descents.
- **Calf raises, straight and bent-knee** (single-leg straight-knee raises in Strength A, single-leg bent-knee raises in Strength B): the soleus, which bent-knee raises target, does a big share of the work in long-distance walking.
- **Knee-to-wall ankle mobility** (daily): more ankle bend means more control on steep ground. You'll track it as a test.

## McGill-style work, later

Stuart McGill's research on spine stability is behind the best-known trio of "spine-sparing" core exercises: the **curl-up**, the **side bridge** and the **bird dog** (often called "the McGill big three"). One study compared variations and progressions of all three. Bracing increased oblique activity, and adding limb movement to the bird dog raised activity in many trunk muscles ([McGill & Karpowicz, 2009](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22McGill%20SM%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22core%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22endurance%20times%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22stability%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=10)) **[Moderate evidence]**. Six weeks of isometric (hold-based) core training increased passive torso stiffness more than dynamic training ([Lee & McGill, 2015](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22McGill%20SM%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22core%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22endurance%20times%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22stability%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=10)) **[Moderate evidence]**. That's why the plan favours **short, high-quality holds** over endless crunches.

| Exercise | Gate | Why | How you'll dose it |
|---|---|---|---|
| McGill curl-up | Now (uninjured hand under your back, injured arm on your chest) | Anti-extension without much spine bending | Phases 2a–2: 3 × 5 holds of 8–10 s |
| Plank | Full clearance | Anti-extension under load | Phase 4a: 3 × 20 s on the forearms; Phase 4 (Fridays): 3 × 40 s, building to 60 s |
| Side bridge (side plank) | Full clearance (weight goes through the forearm) | Anti-lateral-flexion; quadratus lumborum and obliques | Phase 5 (Fridays): 2 × 30 s per side; start from the knees |
| Bird dog | Full clearance (hands-and-knees position) | Anti-rotation and anti-extension with limb movement | Optional from Phase 4: 2 × 6 per side with 5–10 s holds |

A common McGill-style way to dose holds is a descending set: for example 5 holds, then 3, then 1, each about 10 s. It builds endurance without fatigue wrecking form **[Expert opinion]**.

## Rotation and the transverse plane

Most gym training happens front to back (the sagittal plane: squats, deadlifts, presses). Real life and rough trails also happen side to side (frontal plane) and in rotation (transverse plane). Your plan trains all three: rotation first as something to resist, later as something to produce.

**How rotation enters your plan:**

| Gate | Tool | Dose | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Now | Single-leg RDL, split squats, step-ups | In Phases 0–1 | Resisting rotation through the hips and trunk |
| Grip cleared | Pallof press (cable) | Phases 2a–2: 2 × 8–10 per side with 2-s holds | Pure anti-rotation |
| Grip cleared | Cable woodchop, high-to-low and low-to-high | Light from Phase 2a; Phase 3: 3 × 8 per side, plus circuits | Controlled rotation through hips and upper back together |
| Full clearance | Medicine-ball slams, sandbag to shoulder, battle ropes, Turkish get-ups | From Phase 4: circuits and Simple & Sinister | Producing and absorbing force in all planes |

**Cues for the woodchop:** arms long, turn the hips and upper back together, and keep the lower back still. Think "rotate around a stiff middle", not "twist the spine".

## From core to carries

Loaded carries are the most functional core training there is. In McGill's study of strongman events, which included the farmer's walk and suitcase carry, the carrying events "challenged different abilities than the lifting events". The authors suggested loaded carrying could enhance a lifting-based programme. They also found that stiffening the torso helped buttress weaker joints, and in the heavy yoke walk the quadratus lumborum (a deep side-back muscle) worked hard to support the trunk and pelvis side to side ([McGill et al., 2009](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22strongman%20events%22%20AND%20AUTH:%22McGill%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**.

| Carry | Main core job | Gate | First appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suitcase carry, each hand | Anti-lateral-flexion: don't lean away from the weight | Grip cleared, injured-hand grip at least 70% of your uninjured hand, physio OK | Phase 2 (same weight both sides) |
| Farmer's carry | Upright posture, grip, breathing under load | Light from 70%, moderate from 80% (Grip cleared); heavy from 90% (Full clearance) | Phase 3 |
| Sandbag bear-hug carry | Anterior core, upper back, breathing while squeezed | Full clearance | Phase 4a (light), then Phase 4 circuits |
| Sandbag shoulder carry | Asymmetric load: lateral and rotational core | Full clearance | Phase 5 |
| Loaded pack | All of the above, for hours | Light use / rehab started (light) → Full clearance (loaded) | Phase 1 once Light use / rehab started is set → loaded from Phase 4 |

**The carrying rule, briefly** (its home is [Your Arm](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule)):
- **Training carries:** none until Grip cleared, not even with your uninjured hand, then the grip ladder.
- **Daily life until Light use / rehab started:** nothing in your injured hand or on that side's shoulder. A light bag (3 kg or less) on the shoulder of your uninjured side, or a waist pack, is fine; put it on and take it off with your uninjured hand. On walks, a waist pack only (2 kg or less).
- **From Light use / rehab started:** a light day pack on both shoulders, 2–4 kg to start, hip belt fastened, put on seated, uninjured arm first.

Walking with a weight while one arm can't catch you is a fall risk you don't need. Technique, the grip ladder and the full staged path are in [Farmer Strength](09-farmer-strength.md).

## Common mistakes and fixes

- **Holding your breath on every rep.** Fix: breathe out on the effort, and count your breaths during holds.
- **Sucking the belly in.** Fix: brace in 360° instead, as if you're about to be poked.
- **Arching on dead bugs.** Fix: reach the leg less far, or keep the knee bent, until your lower back stays heavy on the mat.
- **Chasing longer holds with bad form.** Fix: stop the set when the position changes; 20 good seconds beats 60 sloppy ones.
- **Doing balance work in the middle of the gym floor.** Fix: always beside a rack or wall your uninjured hand can touch.
- **Leaning on carries.** Fix: lighten the load. Stand as if a string pulls the top of your head up, shoulders down and level.

## Your actions this week

- **Practise the 90/90 breath** every day in your mobility routine: 4 seconds in, 6 seconds out.
- **Rebuild the brace on your tucked hollow holds:** a moderate brace, then keep counting out loud for the whole hold.
- **Rehearse getting down to the mat and up again** beside a bench with no help from your injured arm, before you need it mid-session ([chapter 07](07-mobility.md#getting-to-and-from-the-floor)).
- **Take your hollow-hold and single-leg balance baselines** on Progress → Tests: the balance test next to a support, the hollow hold on the mat after the warm-up's 90/90 breathing.
- **Keep dead bugs slow:** 3 seconds out, 3 seconds back, lower back heavy throughout.
