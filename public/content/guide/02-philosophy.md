# The Philosophy — What We Take From Coaches and Researchers

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - The coaches and researchers this programme draws on agree on more than they disagree: practise strength as a skill, build a big aerobic base, carry heavy things, stay consistent, and treat recovery as part of training.
> - We borrow their **principles**, not their **volumes**. They're elite athletes or have decades of training behind them. This programme is for an experienced lifter returning after an injury layoff: detrained, with a healing arm. You know how to train; the arm sets the pace.
> - Every idea passes the same filter: is it safe for you now, how good is the evidence, and does it serve one of your goals?
> - Each profile is short: who they are, what we use, and what to treat with caution.
> - The chapter ends with the 10 rules that run through your whole programme.

## How to learn from experts

A highlight reel isn't a training plan. Every idea in this chapter goes through three questions:

1. **Is it safe for you now?** If it needs a gate you haven't cleared, it goes on the "later" list.
2. **How good is the evidence?** Some ideas rest on strong research, some on long coaching experience, and some on one person's story. The tags tell you which.
3. **Does it serve one of your five goals?** Functional fitness, all-terrain hiking, core stability, farmer strength and +5 kg of muscle. If an idea serves none of them, it's a distraction.

## Pavel Tsatsouline (StrongFirst)

**Who he is.** The founder of StrongFirst, a "school of strength". StrongFirst describes him as a former Soviet Special Forces physical training instructor, credited with introducing the Russian kettlebell to the West in 1998. His books include *Kettlebell Simple & Sinister* (2013), *Easy Strength* (2011, with Dan John) and *The Naked Warrior* (2003).

**Core ideas.**

- **Strength is a skill,** built by regular, crisp practice rather than cramming, and "Reaching your goals doesn't have to hurt."
- **Grease the groove (GTG):** short, frequent, submaximal practice sets, never taken to failure.
- **Tension and attention:** creating and controlling whole-body tension is a skill in itself.
- **Simple, repeatable programmes.** *Simple & Sinister* is 100 one-arm kettlebell swings in 5 minutes, then 10 get-ups in 10 minutes. The men's "Simple" standard is a 32 kg kettlebell for both lifts, and "Sinister" is 48 kg.

**What we use.** Every working set ends with reps in reserve (usually 2–3), with no grinding. Frequent, easy balance practice in your daily mobility routine. Moderate bracing, breathing out on the effort. Simple sessions you repeat for months. *Simple & Sinister* is a long-term goal after Full clearance.

**Treat with caution.** Swings and get-ups need Full clearance, and get-ups put weight through the wrist. Not every gym has kettlebells, so use the alternatives in the exercise library. Much of the system rests on coaching experience rather than trials. **[Expert opinion]**

## Pat McNamara (TMACS)

**Who he is.** TMACS stands for Tactics, Marksmanship, Adventure, Concepts and Security. The TMACS site says he spent 22 years in US Army special operations units and retired as a Sergeant Major. His books are *T.A.P.S.* and *Sentinel*.

**Core ideas.** "When you're all out of motivation, you have to rely on discipline" (from a podcast interview). Performance over vanity. Simple training, done consistently. Preparedness without paranoia.

**What we use.** We measure what you can *do*: carry, climb, balance and hike. Discipline becomes a system: your minimum standards and minimum session ([chapter 20](20-habits-and-mindset.md)). Preparedness means planning the route, checking the weather and carrying the kit ([chapter 12](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md)).

**Treat with caution.** Special-operations fitness assumes a durable body with nothing healing. Yours is well trained but detrained, and one arm is still healing. Any recovery protocols, medicines or "recovery compounds" you hear about on podcasts stay out of this programme and go through your doctors.

## Peter Attia

**Who he is.** A physician who hosts *The Peter Attia Drive* podcast and wrote *Outlive: The Science and Art of Longevity* (2023) with Bill Gifford.

**Core ideas.**

- **Longevity is lifespan plus healthspan:** living longer, and living better.
- **Four pillars of exercise:** stability, strength, aerobic (Zone 2) work, and anaerobic or VO2max (Zone 5) work.
- **The Centenarian Decathlon:** list the physical tasks you want to do late in life, then work backwards ("backcasting").
- **Lower the barrier to entry.** His site summarises a large analysis in which untrained adults got most of the strength benefit from two moderate sessions a week. **[Moderate evidence]** That was in untrained people, so for you it's an argument for the minimum session on bad weeks, not a plan.

**What we use.** Your functional decathlon ([chapter 01](01-assessment-and-goals.md)). All four pillars every week: stability in every warm-up, strength three times a week, Zone 2 most days, and intervals on low-fall-risk indoor machines when your programme and readiness allow.

**Treat with caution.** **Zone 2 isn't magic:** a 2025 review concluded that current evidence doesn't support Zone 2 as *the* optimal intensity, and that higher intensities matter. **[Moderate evidence]** We use lots of it because it's safe, repeatable and low in fall risk, and hiking demands it ([chapter 10](10-engine-cardio.md)). The headline data linking fitness and grip to lifespan are observational. **[Moderate evidence]** Medicine stays with your doctors.

## Andy Galpin

**Who he is.** A PhD exercise physiologist. His site says he has been Executive Director of the Human Performance Center at Parker University since 2024, after 13 years running a lab at California State University, Fullerton. He hosts *Perform with Dr. Andy Galpin* and recorded a six-part series on Huberman Lab in 2023.

**Core ideas.**

- **Nine adaptations,** from skill and speed to hypertrophy and long-duration endurance. Decide which you want, then train and test for those.
- **Assess before you prescribe,** and retest.
- **"3 to 5" for strength:** 3–5 exercises, reps, sets, minutes of rest and days a week.
- **"Volume is the primary driver in hypertrophy."**
- **Adherence and progressive overload** are the big principles, and **recovery is "a skill that can and should be trained."**

**What we use.** Testing every 4 weeks. Enough weekly sets at the RIR your programme prescribes ([chapter 08](08-building-muscle.md)). Double progression. Recovery as a trainable skill: sleep, breathing and the readiness rule.

**Treat with caution.** "3 to 5" is heavy strength work, so it applies later, to cleared lifts, and never with breath-hold straining before clearance. Galpin is relaxed about training to failure; while you're coming back from a layoff with a healing arm, you stay short of it, at 1–2 reps in reserve at the closest ([chapter 04](04-the-program.md) says where).

## Andrew Huberman

**Who he is.** A professor of neurobiology and ophthalmology at Stanford University School of Medicine, and host of the Huberman Lab podcast since 2021.

**Ideas we use.**

- **Morning light:** outside within 30–60 minutes of waking, for about 10 minutes on bright days, 20 on cloudy days and 30–60 when it's very overcast.
- **A consistent wake time,** every day.
- **Caffeine timing:** none within 8–10 hours of bedtime.
- **Cyclic sighing:** in a randomised trial co-authored by Huberman, 5 minutes a day of breathwork for a month improved mood more than mindfulness meditation, and cyclic sighing (long exhales) did best. **[Moderate evidence]** Two to five minutes after training, or on stressful days: breathe in through the nose, top up with a second short sniff, then breathe out slowly through the mouth.

**Treat with caution.** Many protocols rest on small studies or mechanisms, so treat them as optional experiments. **[Expert opinion]** Episodes carry sponsors, and the sleep toolkit includes an affiliate supplement link. A 2026 umbrella review found cold-water immersion after exercise impaired strength and muscle gains, though certainty was low. **[Moderate evidence]** Sleep supplements are optional at best ([chapter 16](16-supplements.md)).

## Bill Maeda

**Who he is.** A trainer whose home workouts, posted during the 2020 lockdowns, built a large social-media following. His website describes an old football injury that left screws in his shoulder, and a comeback from stage 3 colon cancer and a period of depression. The site lists no formal certifications.

**Core ideas** (from his long-form podcast appearances): slow reps that make light weights feel heavy, breathing well while training, rotation and oblique work, and unconventional lifts alongside the basics.

**What we use.** Tempo: a 3-second lowering on most lifts (`3-1-1-0`), which makes moderate loads count while yours are coming back up. Exhaling through the hard part of every rep. Rotation and anti-rotation core work once cleared (Pallof press and woodchops at Grip cleared). And his story: metalwork in his shoulder, a long road back, and training as a lifeline.

**Treat with caution.** His expertise is experience-based. **[Expert opinion]** Unstable surfaces, fast-feet drills and club or mace swinging are off the menu before Full clearance.

## Josh Bryant and Tom Haviland

**Who they are.** Josh Bryant is a strength coach described in podcast show notes as a record-setting powerlifter and strongman. He co-founded Jailhouse Strong, and his books include *Jailhouse Strong* (2013) and *Built to the Hilt* (2014). Tom Haviland is a strength athlete whose training with Bryant is widely discussed on strength podcasts; his background is (not verified).

**Core ideas (Bryant).** "Gas Station Ready": be ready for whatever physical challenge everyday life throws at you. Methods that adapt to the person. Rucking, and hill sprints.

**What we use.** "Gas Station Ready" as the working definition of "very functional". Rucking for your hiking goal: light rucking from Light use / rehab started, and loaded rucking at Full clearance. Hills on the stair climber and incline treadmill now; hill sprints wait for Full clearance.

**Treat with caution.** Elite strength numbers aren't comparable with yours, and viral feats are often debated. Benchmark against your last 4-week test, not anyone's highlight reel, including your own from before the injury.

## Joe Rogan and his guests

The Joe Rogan Experience has hosted several of the people in this chapter, including Galpin (#996), Pavel (#1399) and Attia (#1961). Long-form conversation is a great way to meet ideas, but it isn't peer review, and guests' claims vary widely in evidence. **[Expert opinion]**

- **Cameron Hanes,** bowhunter and ultramarathon runner ("Nobody Cares. Work Harder."; books *Endure* and *Undeniable*). **Use:** show up and put in the work, and train with a purpose: his fitness serves long mountain days with a pack, close to your goal. **Caution:** "no excuses" can slide into ignoring pain, and right now pain is information.
- **Jocko Willink,** retired US Navy SEAL officer (*Extreme Ownership*, with Leif Babin, and *Discipline Equals Freedom: Field Manual*). **Use:** ownership of your physio homework, logging, sleep and food; a fixed routine frees you from deciding every morning. **Caution:** discipline isn't punishment. Protect your 7.5-hour sleep window.
- **David Goggins,** retired Navy SEAL and endurance athlete (*Can't Hurt Me* and *Never Finished*); his site describes a record of 4,030 pull-ups in 17 hours. **Use:** you can do hard things, in safe forms, like a long Zone 2 walk in the rain on a safe path. **Caution:** his style is the opposite of what fracture healing needs. Pain in your arm is a stop signal, not a test of character ([chapter 19](19-injury-prevention.md)).

## Where they agree

| Principle | Who says it | Evidence | In your programme |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consistency beats intensity | Galpin, Pavel, McNamara, Hanes | **[Moderate evidence]** from habit research ([chapter 20](20-habits-and-mindset.md)) | Minimum standards, the minimum session, never miss twice |
| Quality reps, short of failure | Pavel, Galpin, Maeda | **[Expert opinion]** | RIR 2–3, a 3-second lowering, double progression |
| A big aerobic base plus some hard work | Attia, Galpin | **[Moderate evidence]** that higher fitness is linked with a longer life; the best mix is still debated | Zone 2 most days, intervals when your programme and readiness allow |
| Stability first | Attia, Pavel, Maeda | **[Expert opinion]** | Balance in the daily routine, core in every strength session |
| Carry heavy things, eventually | Attia, Pavel, Bryant | **[Moderate evidence]** that grip strength is a health marker | Legs and trunk now, carries as gates open |
| Recovery is training | Galpin, Huberman, Attia | **[Expert opinion]** | The readiness rule, sleep window, deloads |
| Ownership and discipline | McNamara, Willink, Goggins | **[Expert opinion]** | Daily check-in, weekly review, physio homework |

## Our 10 rules

1. **The gates are law.** Your fracture clinic and physio outrank everyone in this chapter, and this guide. If a gate isn't cleared, the answer is no.
2. **Strength is a skill.** Practise it often and crisply, with reps in reserve (usually 2–3). Never grind.
3. **Slow down to get strong.** A 3-second lowering and control beat heavier loads.
4. **Build the engine.** Lots of Zone 2, plus a little hard work when your programme allows and you're GREEN.
5. **Stability first.** Balance, breathing and single-leg control come before load.
6. **Carry things.** Legs and trunk now, hands when cleared. Progress carries like a strength lift.
7. **Show up, scale down.** Do the minimum session on bad days, and never miss twice.
8. **Measure, don't guess.** Readiness daily, review weekly, test every 4 weeks.
9. **Recover like it's training.** Sleep, food (no dieting while the bone heals) and the readiness rule.
10. **Borrow principles, not volumes.** You know how to train; the arm sets the pace. Admire the elite, and judge yourself against your logbook, not your old bests.
