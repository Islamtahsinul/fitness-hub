# Sleep, Recovery & Readiness

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - Aim for **7.5–9 hours in bed**. If you train at about 06:00, that means **lights out at about 21:15** and the same wake time every day, weekends included.
> - Every morning the hub turns your check-in into **RED, AMBER or GREEN**, and you train accordingly, even when years of training instinct say you could do more. The rule below is exactly the one the hub applies, including RED at arm pain of 7/10 or more and AMBER for rising arm pain or a fasting day.
> - **Resting HR:** log a **60-second manual pulse** from your first check-in. If you change method (for example from a wearable's overnight number to the manual pulse, or later to a chest strap), press **"I changed how I measure resting HR"** in Settings so readiness re-baselines.
> - **Trackers are optional.** The manual pulse (free) covers resting HR, a **chest strap** covers Zone 2, and a wearable band is worth it only if you want sleep data. If you stop using a wearable, export its data first.
> - Use a **smart scale for weight trends only**. Its body-fat % isn't reliable enough to act on.

## Why recovery matters more on the way back

You know the basics: you adapt **between** sessions, mostly while you sleep. Two things make recovery harder than you remember. You're detrained after the layoff, and your body is also healing a fracture. Good sleep and good food are the two biggest levers you control.

Sleep loss hits muscle building fast. In one lab study, a single night without sleep cut muscle protein synthesis by **18%**, raised cortisol by **21%** and lowered testosterone by **24%**. The authors concluded that one night "is sufficient to induce anabolic resistance". **[Moderate evidence]**, from a small study of 13 young adults.

## How much sleep you need

- The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society recommend that adults "sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis", and add that more than 9 hours may suit young adults and people recovering from sleep debt or illness. **[Strong evidence]**
- The NHS says most adults need **7–9 hours**.
- You're training six mornings a week, building muscle and healing a bone, so aim for **7.5–9 hours in bed**. **[Expert opinion]**
- The hub flags **under 6 hours** as an AMBER day.

## A bedtime that supports early training

Work backwards from your wake time, allowing about 45 minutes for water, a snack and the journey.

| Session starts | Wake | Lights out (8 hours in bed) | Start winding down |
|---|---|---|---|
| 06:00 | 05:15 | 21:15 | 20:15 |
| 06:30 | 05:45 | 21:45 | 20:45 |
| 07:00 | 06:15 | 22:15 | 21:15 |

**Make it stick:**

- **Keep the same wake time 7 days a week.** The NHS advises waking and getting up at the same time every day. A lie-in of up to an hour at weekends is a reasonable compromise. **[Expert opinion]**
- **Wind down for an hour:** dim lights, a shower, reading, or the 90/90 breathing drill from your mobility routine. Every Mind Matters suggests no phones, tablets or computers in that hour.
- **Keep the bedroom dark, quiet and cool,** with blackout curtains, an eye mask or earplugs (NHS). Towns and cities are rarely dark or quiet, so the mask and earplugs earn their keep.
- **Eat your last big meal at about 18:30.** A pre-bed milk or yoghurt is fine (chapter 15).
- **Worried thoughts at bedtime?** Write tomorrow's to-do list before bed (Every Mind Matters). If the worries are about your arm, write them as questions for the clinic.

> [!SAFETY]
> **Night-time falls.** If you train early, you'll be up in the dark for much of the winter, with only your uninjured hand to catch yourself. Put plug-in night lights on the route to the bathroom, keep the floor clear, and switch on a light before you move. If your arm aches at night, ask your clinic how best to position and support it in bed.

## Light: setting your body clock

Bright light in the morning makes you alert and helps you feel sleepy at the right time in the evening, while bright light late in the evening pushes sleep later. **[Moderate evidence]** Andrew Huberman's protocol, **[Expert opinion]** for the exact doses, is to get outside within 30–60 minutes of waking for about **10 minutes on a clear day, 20 when it's cloudy and 30–60 when it's very overcast**, and to avoid bright overhead light late at night.

**The winter problem:** from about November to February in the UK, it's still dark when you train early. So put bright lights on as soon as you get up, get outside in daylight after your session or at lunchtime, and use lamps rather than overhead lights from about 20:00. On dark or icy mornings, walk on the treadmill rather than outside: fall risk comes first.

## Caffeine, alcohol and naps

- **Caffeine:** in a sleep study, 400 mg of caffeine reduced measured sleep **even when taken 6 hours before bed**, and people didn't notice the effect. **[Moderate evidence]** The NHS advises no tea or coffee in the 6 hours before bed, and Huberman suggests 8–10. So have your **last caffeine about 8 hours before lights out** (by 13:00–13:30 for a 21:15 lights out), including tea, cola and energy drinks. Doses are in [chapter 16](16-supplements.md).
- **Alcohol** disrupts sleep. Every Mind Matters says alcohol and nicotine "can also have disruptive effects on sleep" ([chapter 14](14-nutrition.md)).
- **Naps:** a meta-analysis found short daytime naps improve alertness, with early-afternoon naps working best. **[Moderate evidence]** After a short night, 10–30 minutes before about 15:00 is fine. If you struggle to fall asleep at night, skip naps for a couple of weeks. **[Expert opinion]**

## Stress

Your body doesn't keep separate accounts for training stress and life stress: work, money, worry about your arm and poor sleep all draw on the same recovery budget. **[Expert opinion]** Five slow 90/90 breaths with long exhales, easy walks on good paths, a to-do list before bed and talking to someone you trust all help. [Chapter 20](20-habits-and-mindset.md) covers stress, burnout and when to get support.

## The readiness rule

Every morning the hub combines your check-in into a colour. This is the rule, exactly as the hub applies it, on every page. A weight on its own doesn't count as a check-in, so fill in the rest.

| Colour | When | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **RED** | **Any arm red flag is ticked, or arm pain is 7/10 or more** | "Stop. No training today, including your physio exercises, until you've been advised. Contact your fracture clinic today, or NHS 111 / 999 as the red-flag guidance says." The hub switches off add-ons and the Start buttons |
| **AMBER** | **Any one of these:** morning resting HR at least 5 bpm above your 7-day average (once there are 5 or more readings since your last change of method); under 6 hours of sleep; arm pain 4/10 or more; arm pain 2 or more points above its 7-day average (with at least 3 earlier readings); soreness 7/10 or more; energy 2/5 or less; a date inside one of your fasting periods | "Easy day: halve the sets, Zone 2 only, no intervals, hold loads" |
| **GREEN** | None of the above | Train as planned |

**Rising arm pain is a warning, not just a number.** If your arm has sat at 1/10 all week and today it's 3/10, that's AMBER even though 3 is below the 4/10 line, and the hub adds: "Rising arm pain can be the red flag 'pain getting worse'. If it keeps rising, or you're unsure, call your fracture clinic today." The arm red flags are listed, with what each means, in [chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md). In an emergency, call 999.

**GREEN means train.** The hub's coach notes never suggest an easy day on a GREEN morning; at most they point out a trend, such as resting HR creeping up over a fortnight.

**How to run an AMBER day:**

- **Halve the sets,** rounding up: three planned sets become two, and two become one. Keep the same exercises.
- **Hold your loads.** Same weights as last time, stopping 3–4 reps short. The hub shows "Hold: same load, RIR 3–4" and doesn't suggest increases.
- **Zone 2 only.** An intervals day becomes easy Zone 2 on the bike or treadmill.
- **Do your mobility routine as normal,** and **eat normally.** An easy day is not a diet day.

**When AMBER keeps coming back:** look for the cause: sleep, illness, stress or a jump in training. If you're AMBER on **3 or more days in a week**, or your main lifts drop two sessions running, **take your next deload early** (see "Deloads" below: the hub suggests it but won't do it for you). See your GP if you feel unwell.

## Your comeback: patience beats ego

If you've trained for years, you know what hard training feels like and what you used to recover from. That memory is the risk now. Your head remembers your old numbers and your old recovery; your body is detrained and healing a fracture. For the first few weeks, expect the same session to cost you more recovery than it did before the injury. **[Expert opinion]**

- **Let the colour decide, not your pride.** An AMBER day isn't a verdict on your toughness. Halve the sets and hold the loads, even when the warm-up feels great.
- **Let the loads come back through the logbook.** Where you have no logged history, the plan starts around a load you could do for 15, and double progression finds your level within 2 sessions ([chapter 04](04-the-program.md)). Strength you've had before returns quickly that way; testing your old max doesn't speed it up.
- **Take every deload,** and take one early when the rule says so. If a deload week feels too easy, it's working.
- **The arm outranks everything.** Rising arm pain or any red flag ends the session, however good the rest of you feels.

You know how to train; the arm sets the pace. [Chapter 20](20-habits-and-mindset.md) covers the mindset side of coming back.

## Your morning measurements

### Resting heart rate: the 60-second pulse

Your morning resting heart rate is your simplest recovery gauge. It tends to rise when you're ill, short of sleep, dehydrated or stressed, or after a very hard day. **[Expert opinion]**

1. **Measure as soon as you wake,** before getting up, before caffeine and before checking your phone.
2. **Lie still for a minute,** then count your pulse for a **full 60 seconds**. Use the first two fingertips of your **uninjured** hand, pressing gently on one side of your neck beside the windpipe. Don't use the injured hand, and don't press on both sides of the neck at once.
3. Do it the same way every day, and **log it in the hub's check-in.**

The hub compares today's pulse with the **average of the previous 7 days**. The resting-HR part of the rule only counts once there are **5 or more readings since your last change of method**; until then, your colour comes from your other answers.

### Changing how you measure: press the button

Different methods give different numbers. Many wearables report a resting HR taken during your sleep, and a pulse taken in bed after waking usually reads a few beats higher **[Expert opinion]**. If the hub compared a new method with an average built on the old one, you'd get false AMBER days.

- **The morning you switch,** whether from a wearable's number to the manual pulse or later to a band or chest strap, go to **Settings** and press **"I changed how I measure resting HR"**.
- From that date the hub leaves resting HR out of your colour until it has 5 readings taken the new way, then compares like with like. Sleep, arm pain, soreness, energy and fasting days count as normal throughout.
- If the hub's resting-HR field has only ever held your manual pulse, there's nothing to press, because nothing changed.

### HRV: optional

Heart rate variability (HRV) measures how much the gap between heartbeats varies. Higher than your own normal usually means you're well recovered.

- The hub stores HRV, but **it doesn't use it to set your readiness colour.** Treat it as context.
- A review found that adjusting training based on HRV improved fitness over a fixed plan "only by a small margin". **[Moderate evidence]** Your resting-HR rule does the job.
- **HRV numbers from different devices aren't interchangeable.** If you change device, start a fresh baseline.

## Trackers: what you need and what's optional

### If you're giving up a wearable

1. **Export your data first.** Most wearable apps have an export option in their settings or help pages. Use it before your account or subscription ends, and save the files somewhere safe, such as a folder next to your hub backups. If there's no export option, you can ask any company for a copy of your personal data with a subject access request; the Information Commissioner's Office says organisations "usually have one month to respond".
2. **Run a 2–4 week overlap,** from your first check-in until you stop wearing it:
  - Every morning, take the **60-second manual pulse** and log it as your resting HR in the hub. That's the method you'll keep, so the hub's 7-day average stays consistent when the wearable goes.
  - Log the wearable's **HRV** in the hub's HRV field, and write its **resting HR** in the check-in notes. Many wearables take resting HR during your sleep, not from a pulse on waking ([chapter 10](10-engine-cardio.md) has the study), so expect the two numbers to differ.
  - Already been typing the wearable's number into the resting-HR field? Switch to the manual pulse now, and press **"I changed how I measure resting HR"** in Settings that morning.
3. **Set your baseline.** After at least 14 mornings, work out the average of your manual readings and enter it in Settings as your resting-HR baseline. It's the reference for your resting-HR goal in [chapter 01](01-assessment-and-goals.md).
4. **Write the summary in your weekly review:** "Baseline, from __ to __: manual resting HR __ bpm (range __ to __). Wearable resting HR __ bpm, HRV __ ms."

> [!TIP]
> Why log the method you'll keep from day one, when the button exists? The button protects your readiness colour when you switch, but it can't make old and new numbers comparable. One method from the start keeps months of resting-HR history usable for trends, including your 12-week goal.

### The cheapest accurate options

| Option | Rough cost | What it's good for | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **60-second manual pulse** | Free | Morning resting HR, which is all the readiness rule needs | A minute a day, and you have to be consistent. No exercise or sleep data |
| **Chest strap**, paired with your phone or the gym's cardio machines | For example, a Polar H10 was £69.20 at Polar UK (checked November 2026); cheaper Bluetooth straps from other brands cost less (not verified) | Accurate heart rate in Zone 2, intervals and the drift test in [chapter 10](10-engine-cardio.md). Can also record morning HRV with an app | Has to go round your chest (see below), and the strap needs rinsing after use |
| **Phone-camera HRV app** | Free to a few pounds (not verified) | Morning HRV, if you want to keep tracking it | A fingertip over the camera for a minute or so, mornings only |
| **Budget fitness band** | Roughly £25–60 (not verified) | Sleep duration, overnight resting-HR trends and steps | Sleep stages are unreliable, exercise HR is less accurate than a chest strap, and some features need a subscription |

**What the evidence says:**

- **Chest straps are the most accurate everyday option** for exercise heart rate, and wrist sensors lose accuracy on the bike, in gym work and at higher heart rates. Chapter 10 has the studies. **[Strong evidence]**
- **Sleep trackers are good at detecting sleep but poor at detecting wake and stages.** In a test of seven consumer devices against laboratory sleep studies, all detected sleep well (sensitivity 0.93 or more), but detection of wake was low to medium, sleep-stage results were inconsistent, and devices did worse on disrupted nights. **[Moderate evidence]**
- **Phone-camera HRV can work at rest.** In 29 healthy people, a smartphone camera and a chest strap both agreed acceptably with an ECG for a standard HRV measure (rMSSD). **[Moderate evidence]**

**The recommendation:** the manual pulse every morning, plus a chest strap for your Zone 2 and interval sessions. Buy a budget band only if you want sleep numbers; your bedtime and wake time already give you your sleep hours for the check-in. Wear any band on your **uninjured** wrist, and nothing tight around the injured forearm. **[Expert opinion]** Product names are examples, not endorsements.

### Putting on a chest strap one-handed

- With help if needed, set the strap once to a loop that fits snugly just under your chest, and leave it clipped.
- Each time: wet the electrode area, slip the loop over your head or step into it, and slide it into place with your uninjured hand. Clip the sensor on with the same hand.
- Never pull or hold the strap with your injured hand. If you can't manage alone, ask for help until you're cleared. **[Expert opinion]**

### A smart scale: weight trends only

- **Trust the weight, not the body-fat %.** In a study of three smart scales against a DEXA scan, body weight was within about 0–0.3 kg (median error), but fat-mass errors ran from about 2 to 4 kg, and the authors concluded that smart scales "are not accurate for body composition". **[Moderate evidence]** An earlier study found foot-to-foot scales (the usual bathroom-scale design) were less accurate for individuals than devices with hand and foot electrodes, though fine for group averages. **[Moderate evidence]**
- **Ignore** the app's body-fat, muscle-mass, "metabolic age" and BMR figures. Your calorie targets come from the hub, and your muscle progress shows up in your tape measurements, photos and training log ([chapter 08](08-building-muscle.md)).
- **Weigh the same way every morning:** after the toilet, before food or drink, on the same spot of hard floor. Log it in the check-in and judge the 7-day average ([chapter 14](14-nutrition.md)).

## Deloads

Your programme has deload weeks built in, roughly every 5–6 weeks: half the sets of the same exercises at the same loads, with easy intervals ([chapter 04](04-the-program.md) has the weeks). In those planned weeks the hub halves the sets for you.

**The hub never adds or moves a deload by itself.** It suggests one, through the deload rule on the **Programme** page, and the rule is simple: **take one early** if you're AMBER on 3 or more days in a week, or your main lifts drop two sessions running. To take one, train the whole week in easy-day mode: turn on the easy-day adjustments each time you log a session (half the sets, Zone 2 only, same loads), and keep walking and doing your mobility daily.

That matches what coaches do: in a study of strength coaches, deloads typically lasted **5–7 days every 4–6 weeks**, mainly by cutting sets and reps and taking sets less close to failure. **[Expert opinion]**, because this is survey evidence about what coaches do, not trials.

During a deload, **keep eating your surplus** and your full protein. The work eases off, but the healing and rebuilding don't.

## Soreness

- After a long layoff, even lifts you know well will make you sore for a day or three at first, which is why Re-entry Week is deliberately conservative. **[Expert opinion]** Soreness of 7/10 or more makes it an AMBER day.
- **What helps:** easy movement, sleep, protein and time.
- **Soreness or injury?** Soreness is a dull, spread-out ache in the muscle that eases as you warm up. Sharp pain, joint pain, swelling, or pain that gets worse during a set means stop that exercise. Follow the pain rules in [chapter 19](19-injury-prevention.md).

> [!SAFETY]
> **Dark urine after training needs same-day advice.** Dark (cola-coloured) urine with severe muscle pain or swelling after training can be a sign of rhabdomyolysis, a rare but serious breakdown of muscle after unaccustomed hard work. Get medical advice the same day: your GP or NHS 111, or 999 if you feel very unwell. The risk is highest in your first couple of weeks back, when your drive and technique are ahead of what detrained muscles can take. It's one reason Re-entry Week is deliberately conservative.

> [!WARNING]
> **Painkillers and bone healing.** Ask your fracture clinic before taking anti-inflammatories such as ibuprofen or naproxen for training soreness. Meta-analyses link these drugs with delayed healing or nonunion, mainly with longer use; short courses of under two weeks didn't show a significant risk in a review of randomised trials. **[Moderate evidence]** Your clinic will tell you which painkiller suits you.

## Illness: the neck check

These rules match your programme's. **[Expert opinion]**

| Symptoms | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Above the neck only**: runny or blocked nose, sneezing, a mild sore throat, and no fever | An easy Zone 2 walk at most. Stop if you feel worse |
| **Below the neck or all over**: fever or chills, aching muscles, chest symptoms, vomiting or diarrhoea | Rest. Restart 24–48 hours after symptoms clear, with a deload-style week |

There's some evidence behind it: in a study of about 7,000 runners, those who started a race with whole-body symptoms were about **twice as likely not to finish**. **[Moderate evidence]**, from an observational study.

> [!SAFETY]
> **After a fracture, a fever is also an arm red flag.** Especially after an open fracture or surgery, a fever or feeling generally unwell can be a sign of infection. It makes your day RED: contact the fracture clinic or NHS 111. Don't just treat it as a cold.

## Cold and heat

- **No ice baths after lifting.** In a 12-week study, young men who sat in 10°C water after each strength session gained about a third of the thigh muscle of those who did an easy cool-down instead (103 g against 309 g). **[Moderate evidence]** If you ever use cold exposure, keep it away from strength days.
- **No ice baths, cold plunges or sauna until full clearance and a healed wound.** You'd be getting in and out of slippery tubs one-handed, with a risk of cold shock or light-headedness. A brief cool shower is fine if you're steady on your feet. **[Expert opinion]**
- **Sauna later:** a large Finnish study linked frequent sauna use with lower death rates, but that's an association, not proof. **[Moderate evidence]** There's no good evidence that sauna builds muscle.

## Recovery after long hikes

- **Refuel within 1–2 hours** with carbohydrate and protein, and eat more that day and the next. Fuelling during the walk follows the one long-walk rule in [chapter 14](14-nutrition.md).
- **Rehydrate,** with some salt. Losing more than 2% of your body weight (about 1.6 kg for the 80 kg example profile) means you should drink more next time (ACSM).
- **Check your feet,** and deal with hot spots and blisters early.
- **Next day:** an easy walk or bike session and your mobility routine. If your legs are 7/10 sore or more, it's an AMBER day.
- [Chapters 11](11-hiking-training.md) and [13](13-uk-hiking-roadmap.md) build easier days around your hikes.

## Fasting and sleep

If you fast, for example in Ramadan or during another religious fast, add the dates under **Settings → Fasting periods**. Every fasting day is then AMBER automatically, so the hub runs easy days for you. Pre-dawn and late meals cut into sleep, and studies in Ramadan find sleep gets shorter, so protect a fixed sleep window, consider a short early-afternoon nap and keep caffeine to early evening. Eating, training after you break your fast and the dizziness stop rule are in [chapter 14](14-nutrition.md).

## Training and your mental health

Physical activity has medium-sized benefits for depression, anxiety and distress. **[Strong evidence]** A serious injury can knock your mood and confidence, and training, routine and visible progress in the hub help. If you feel low or anxious most days for more than two weeks, talk to your GP. If you ever feel unable to cope, call Samaritans free, day or night, on **116 123**. [Chapter 20](20-habits-and-mindset.md) has more.
