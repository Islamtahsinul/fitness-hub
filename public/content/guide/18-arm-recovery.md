# Your Arm — Fracture Recovery & Return to Loading

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - Your fracture clinic and physio are in charge of your injured arm. This chapter explains what *usually* happens after an arm fracture (for example a forearm fracture, possibly fixed with plates), so you can ask sharp questions and follow their plan. It never replaces their plan.
> - Your next fracture-clinic appointment is where the gates move. If you haven't been referred to physio yet, **asking for that referral is question 1**.
> - After surgery or an open fracture, infection is the complication to respect most. Redness, heat, discharge, fever or rising pain means phoning the fracture clinic the same day, even months from now. Arm pain of 7/10 or more makes the day RED.
> - Every answer you get maps to a hub gate (see the decision table). Move a gate only on a clear yes. Setting **Light use / rehab started** also unlocks other-arm weights and the good-arm sessions, so set it only if the clinic also said yes to training your other arm.
> - **This chapter is the home of the carrying rule.** Until Light use / rehab started: nothing in the injured hand or on that side's shoulder, and only a waist pack on walks. Training carries wait for Grip cleared.

> [!SAFETY]
> Nothing here is a diagnosis or a treatment plan. If anything in this chapter differs from what your surgeon, clinic or physio tells you, follow them. Then update your gates and physio exercises in the hub.

## Your arm at a glance

| | What to note |
|---|---|
| Injury | Which bone or bones broke, which arm, the date, and whether the fracture was open (the bone came through the skin) or closed |
| Fixation | A cast or splint, or surgery: plates and screws (open reduction and internal fixation, ORIF), a nail or wires |
| Hand dominance | Whether the injured arm is your dominant one. It changes how you read your grip numbers (see the grip ladder below) |
| Clearance | Which hub gates your team has cleared. Until they clear one, all four stay unset |
| Physio | Whether you've been referred, who will contact you, and when |
| Next review | Your next fracture-clinic appointment. Add it under *Appointments* on the *Your arm* page |

Check that Settings shows your **Injured arm** and **Dominant hand**. If the injured arm is your weaker one, daily life is a little easier: your phone, eating, writing and buckles stay with your stronger hand, and that's also the hand that protects you in a stumble. If it's your dominant arm, expect everyday tasks to feel clumsy for a while, and ask your clinic or physio about one-handed tips. Either way, dominance changes how you read your grip numbers (see the grip ladder below).

**Allowed now (no clearance needed):** everything in the programme that leaves the injured arm out of it. That includes leg machines, bodyweight leg work, arm-free core, walking, the stair climber and treadmill (uninjured hand resting lightly on the rail, injured arm across your stomach), the upright bike, and the daily mobility routine ([chapter 07](07-mobility.md)), which ends with the half-kneeling hip-flexor stretch until Light use / rehab started and the couch stretch after it.

**Not yet:** anything that grips, holds, pushes, pulls, braces or takes weight through the injured arm. Also no two-handed equipment, no real fall risks, no maximal breath-holding, and no weights for the other arm (that needs Good arm OK). If the clinic gave you finger, elbow or shoulder exercises earlier, keep doing them and enter them under *Your physio exercises* on the *Your arm* page, but don't invent new ones. The hub's daily physio slot appears once your physio's exercises are entered there and Light use / rehab started is set. Until then the page says "Waiting for your physio's programme".

## Infection: the complication to respect most

If your fracture was open or needed surgery, infection is the thing to watch. "Open" means the broken bone came through the skin, so bacteria could reach it. That's why open fractures get urgent antibiotics and surgical cleaning (the UK standard, BOAST, sets timings for both). Infection can appear months later, even after the wound looked healed, and metalwork such as plates and screws gives bacteria a surface to cling to **[Expert opinion]**. Pus, a sinus (a small channel to the skin) or wound breakdown *confirm* a fracture-related infection (Metsemakers 2018). Fever, drainage, warmth and redness are *suggestive* signs that need checking (Onsea 2022) **[Moderate evidence]**. After a closed fracture treated in a cast, infection is much less likely, but the same signs still mean a same-day call **[Expert opinion]**.

### Who to call, and how fast

| What you notice | Who to contact | How fast |
|---|---|---|
| Possible sepsis: very fast breathing; confusion, slurred speech or not making sense; blue, pale or blotchy skin; a very high or very low temperature, or feeling hot, cold or shivery; a rash that doesn't fade when pressed | **999** or A&E | Now |
| Fingers suddenly pale, blue, grey or cold; a new bend, clunk or "give"; sudden severe numbness; severe pain after a fall | **A&E** (don't drive yourself) or 999 | Now |
| Fever, or feeling generally unwell | **NHS 111** (your fracture clinic in working hours) | Now |
| Redness or heat; fluid, pus or a bad smell from the scar; a wound that reopens; rising pain or new night pain; new swelling; new numbness or tingling; thinning skin over a plate | **Fracture clinic** (NHS 111 out of hours) | The same day |
| A fall or knock onto the arm that feels fine | Fracture clinic | The same or next working day |
| Burning pain with colour, temperature or sweating changes that come and go | Fracture clinic or GP | Within a few days |

> Tick the matching red flag in your morning check-in. The day turns RED: no training at all, including your physio exercises, until you've been advised. The *Your arm* page lists all nine red flags with exactly what to do for each.

### How the hub reads your arm pain

Your morning check-in asks for your arm pain right now, from 0 to 10. The hub reads it like this:

| What you enter | The day | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Any red flag ticked, or arm pain of 7/10 or more | RED | Stop. No training today, including your physio exercises, until you've been advised. Contact your fracture clinic today, or NHS 111 / 999 as the red-flag guidance says |
| Arm pain 2 or more points above its 7-day average (once there are at least 3 earlier readings) | AMBER | An easy day. Rising arm pain can be the red flag "pain getting worse": if it keeps rising, or you're unsure, call your fracture clinic today |

**Rising pain is a red flag in its own right.** The hub can only compare numbers, and it needs a few days of them. If the pain is clearly worse than last week, or it's new at rest or at night, tick the "pain getting worse" red flag yourself and phone the clinic the same day. Don't wait for an average to catch up **[Expert opinion]**.

## Your next fracture-clinic appointment

Fracture clinics are busy and consultations can be short **[Expert opinion]**. Go in with four things: a prioritised question list, the right paperwork, a way to capture the answers, and a table that turns those answers into gates.

### The week before

- Log your arm pain (at rest, with activity and at night) and your swelling score every day on the *Your arm* page, so you can show a trend.
- Find your discharge letter or operation note. It names the exact injury and, if you had surgery, the plates and screws.
- Note any numbness, tingling, stiffness or night pain: which fingers, when, and since when.
- Save the clinic's number in your phone, and add the appointment date, time and place to the hub.

### What to bring

- **The printed questions** (*Your arm* page, *Print the questions*) and a pen.
- **A list of your medicines and supplements,** including painkillers, creatine and vitamin D.
- **Your letters,** discharge summary and any operation note.
- **Dated photos** of the scar if anything has changed, and a printout or phone photo of your pain chart from the *Your arm* page if you've logged a trend.
- **A one-line training summary.** For example: "Gym six days a week: leg machines, bike, stair climber, treadmill. Nothing with my injured arm."
- **Someone to take notes,** if you can. Ask before recording the consultation on your phone.
- **A loose sleeve, and no watch or rings on the injured side.** Many fracture clinics X-ray you first, so arrive early. Bring water and a snack, because clinics often run late.

### The top 8 questions, in order

These are the first group on the *Your arm* page, in the same order. If you only get five minutes, ask the first four.

1. **Physio:** "Can you refer me to physiotherapy or hand therapy today? Who will contact me and when, should I start anything before then, and who do I call if I've heard nothing in two weeks?"
2. **Healing:** "Has the fracture united on today's X-ray? If more than one bone broke, how far along is each one? When is my next X-ray or review?"
3. **Infection:** "Is the skin fully healed, including any wound or scar and the skin over any plates or screws? Is there any sign of infection? Exactly which symptoms should make me call you, NHS 111 or 999? What's your direct number?"
4. **Daily use:** "What can I use my injured arm for right now, such as dressing, typing, carrying a light bag or wearing a light rucksack? Is there a weight limit in kilograms?"
5. **Good arm:** "Can I train my other arm with weights, seated, two or three times a week at moderate effort and without straining? Are there any limits on hard leg training or bike intervals?"
6. **Gripping:** "When can I start gripping and holding light weights with my injured hand, say a 2–5 kg dumbbell? What do you or the physio need to see first?"
7. **Plates (if you have them):** "Are the plates staying in for good, and is anything about them worrying you? A hard fall can break the bone at the end of a plate, so are there any long-term limits I should keep to? If removal were ever suggested, how long would I need to protect the arm afterwards?" If you didn't have surgery, ask instead whether anything about how the bone is healing means long-term limits.
8. **In writing:** "Could you put my restrictions, my permissions and any dates in the clinic letter, and send me a copy?"

**Why physio comes first:** it leads all your rehab, NHS physio can have a long waiting list, and after a forearm fracture, turning the palm and grip strength are the slowest things to return. If they won't refer you, ask why. In some areas you can refer yourself to NHS musculoskeletal (MSK) physio without a GP. If you go private, choose a chartered (MCSP), HCPC-registered physio, ideally one who does hand therapy, and take your clinic letter (NHS). The other 13 questions on the page are for if there's time, or for your first physio session.

### How to record the answers

1. **In the room:** tick each question as you ask it, and write the answer on the printout in their words, with numbers and dates ("light use up to 1 kg", "no gripping until the next review in six weeks"). Paper or phone notes come first; you type them into the hub afterwards.
2. **Before you leave, read it back:** "So from today I can…, and … waits until …. Is that right?" This catches misunderstandings while you can still fix them.
3. **That evening, on the *Your arm* page:**
  - Type each answer into that question's *Their answer* box.
  - Write a three-line summary in the appointment's *Outcome* box: what's healed, what's allowed, what's next.
  - Record any gate (*Record clearance* on that gate's card) exactly as the table below says.
  - Add the next review, and the physio appointment once you have it, under *Appointments*.
  - Enter any home exercises under *Your physio exercises*, exactly as prescribed. That's what makes the daily physio slot appear.
4. **When the letter arrives,** check it against your notes. If they differ, follow the letter, and phone the clinic if anything is unclear.

### Turning answers into hub gates

Gates are cumulative: setting a higher gate also counts for every gate below it. That's why **Light use / rehab started** needs a yes to training your other arm as well: setting it also unlocks everything **Good arm OK** gives, which means other-arm weights, the good-arm sessions and good-hand grip readings. When you press *Record clearance* on the *Your arm* page, the confirm step spells this out before it saves. The hub only accepts a clearance dated today or earlier. If they give you a future date ("from the 15th"), set a phone reminder and record the gate on that day.

The same logic holds for every gate: each one also unlocks everything below it. If they've ruled out something that a gate would unlock, set the lower gate that fits, or nothing, and ask at the next visit.

**If an answer is vague.** "Be sensible" and "let pain guide you" aren't gates. Turn them into yes-or-no questions: "Can I hold a 5 kg dumbbell? Can I push against a wall? Can I carry a 3 kg rucksack on a flat path? Can I hang from a bar with my feet on the floor?" If you're still unsure afterwards, don't move the gate. Ask your physio or phone the clinic.

**If nothing changes,** that's common, especially after an open fracture or when both forearm bones broke. Phase 1 keeps building your legs, engine and core, which is what your hiking goal needs most anyway.

## What the X-ray is looking for

Two X-ray views at right angles show the outer walls (cortices) of the bone from four sides. The clinic checks how many cortices are bridged by new bone (many studies call a fracture united at 3 of 4, as in Ahmed 2025). They also check the alignment, and whether any plate or screw is broken, loose or surrounded by a dark halo, which can mean loosening or infection **[Expert opinion]**. If both forearm bones broke, one can lag behind the other.

## How long healing usually takes

The NHS says a broken arm or wrist usually takes about 6–12 weeks to recover, and longer after a severe injury. Fractures through both forearm shafts sit at the slow end. In plated series, union took a median of 13.6 weeks (Polat 2022), a mean of 13.2 weeks (Köse 2017) and 13.5–15.7 weeks (Rust 2024) **[Moderate evidence]**.

Open fractures are slower on average. In a database of 309,330 fractures, an open fracture raised the odds of non-union by about two-thirds (odds ratio 1.66; Zura 2016). In one recent series of low-grade open forearm fractures, 1.6% became infected and 4.8% didn't unite (Rotman 2025) **[Moderate evidence]**. Most still heal well.

When plates hold the bone very rigidly, it can heal *directly*, laying down new bone without the fluffy callus of textbook pictures (Marsell & Einhorn 2011) **[Moderate evidence]**. So "not much callus" doesn't necessarily mean "not healing". The six healing stages, with typical timings, are on the *Your arm* page.

**Healed is not the same as strong.** About 5 years after both-bone plating, adults had, compared with their other arm, about 75% of the grip, 68–70% of the rotation strength and about 90% of the rotation range (Droll 2007) **[Moderate evidence]**. That's why farmer strength is a months-long project ([chapter 09](09-farmer-strength.md)).

## What physio usually involves, stage by stage

Your physio or hand therapist runs all rehab for the injured arm. Hand therapy combines physiotherapy and occupational therapy skills: scar management, splinting, joint mobilisation, and active and resisted exercise (BSSH). Timings vary widely, and your physio's plan always wins **[Expert opinion]**.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Protect, and move what's allowed | Finger, elbow and shoulder movement, swelling control, wound checks | Now | No injured-arm training. Any home exercises from your team override the programme |
| 2. Restore range | Gentle wrist bending, side-to-side movement, turning the palm up and down, tendon glides, light daily use, scar work | Light use / rehab started | Adds the daily slot "Your physio's programme" once you've entered their exercises on the *Your arm* page: exactly what your physio prescribed. A light day pack and the couch stretch unlock too, along with everything Good arm OK gives |
| 3. Light holds and carries from 70% grip. Still no floor push-ups or planks, hanging or impact |

> [!SAFETY]
> **The arm pain rule (used everywhere in the hub):** follow your physio's limit. If you weren't given one, keep pain at 2/10 or below during the exercise, back to your usual level by the next morning. Never train through pain that's increasing.

Surgeons differ on weight-bearing. In one study, 213 trauma patients bore weight through their arms straight after plating, with 6% complications (Marchand 2021) **[Moderate evidence]**. The hub keeps it at Full clearance unless your physio prescribes it earlier. Never record a higher gate than the one you were actually given.

## Return to lifting, carrying and hanging

There's no single published rule. Clinicians usually look at four things together **[Expert opinion]**:

1. **Clinician clearance,** usually after an X-ray shows the fracture has united.
2. **Range of movement** that's comfortable and close to the other side. Most daily tasks need about 40° of wrist bend each way (Ryu 1991) and about 50° of forearm rotation each way (Morrey 1981) **[Moderate evidence]**. Push-ups, hanging and sandbag carries need much more, so they come last.
3. **Grip compared with the other hand** (the grip ladder below).
4. **Tolerance:** the arm pain rule holds, there's no extra swelling the next morning, and you've passed your physio's functional tests.

### The grip ladder, and why hand dominance matters

The hub measures your injured hand as a percentage of your good hand. It's a rule of thumb **[Expert opinion]**, not a published guideline:
- **At least 70%:** light holds and carries begin, including walking poles. This needs Grip cleared plus your physio's OK.
- **At least 80%:** moderate farmer's carries.
- **At least 90%:** heavy carries, sandbags, loaded rucks and all hanging. This needs Full clearance.

**When the readings open.** On the *Your arm* page, good-hand grip readings unlock at Good arm OK, because a maximal squeeze is training for that arm. Injured-hand readings unlock at Grip cleared, taken by your physio or with their OK. If your physio measures your injured hand's grip before then, write the figure in that appointment's *Outcome* box.

In right-handers, the dominant hand averaged about 13% stronger, while left-handers' hands were about equal (Petersen 1989) **[Moderate evidence]**. Individuals vary, but it changes how you read the ladder:
- **If you're right-handed and the injured hand is your weaker one,** it was probably only about 87–89% of the other before the injury, so the 90% rung means close to a full recovery. Expect it to take longer than the 70% and 80% rungs.
- **If the injured hand is your dominant one,** it was probably the stronger hand before, so reaching 90% doesn't mean you're fully back. Keep building with your physio. If you're left-handed, your hands were probably about equal, so read the rungs at face value.
- **Training your other arm raises the number you're dividing by.** Test both hands on the same day, with the same device and set-up, and not straight after a grip session.
- **If you plateau in the high 80s** and your physio is happy for you to carry heavy or hang, their judgement wins, because anything physio-prescribed overrides the programme. Don't adjust the ladder yourself.

Even NFL players with full-time rehab took about 152 days on average to return to play after forearm plating (Sochacki 2018) **[Moderate evidence]**. For most people, heavy loading builds up over the 6–12 months after the injury **[Expert opinion]**. The farmer path in [chapter 09](09-farmer-strength.md) and the phases in [chapter 04](04-the-program.md) follow these gates.

## The carrying rule

This is the one carrying rule for the whole hub. Every chapter and page that mentions a bag, a pack or a carry follows it. If your clinic or physio gives you a stricter limit, theirs wins.

| What | Until Light use / rehab started | From Light use / rehab started | Later |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Daily life** (shopping, the gym bag, the commute) | Nothing in the injured hand or on that side's shoulder. A light bag (3 kg or less) on the shoulder of your **uninjured** side, or a waist pack, is fine. Put it on and take it off with your uninjured hand | A light day pack on both shoulders, as for walks. What you hold in your injured hand follows exactly what the clinic said, such as "light use up to 1 kg" | Heavier bags in the injured hand follow the grip ladder |
| **On walks** | A waist pack only, 2 kg or less (hike Levels 1–3) | A light day pack on both shoulders. Start at 2–4 kg with the hip belt fastened, and add about 1 kg every 2–3 weeks if the walk and the next morning feel fine (Level 4) | About 10% of your body weight at most until Full clearance (8 kg for an 80 kg adult). Heavier rucking after that ([chapter 11](11-hiking-training.md)) |
| **Training carries** (suitcase, farmer's, sandbag) | None | None | From Grip cleared, by the grip ladder above: light carries from 70% with your physio's OK, moderate farmer's carries from 80%, heavy carries and sandbags from 90% with Full clearance |

**Why so strict?** A load makes a stumble harder to recover from, and a fall is the biggest risk to a healing arm. A strap on the injured side's shoulder also slides down the injured arm and tempts you to grab it with that hand **[Expert opinion]**.

**Putting a day pack on and taking it off (from Light use / rehab started):**
1. Sit on a bench or chair with the pack beside you, straps and hip belt loosened. Never swing a pack up from the floor.
2. Slip your **uninjured arm** through its strap first and settle the pack onto your back.
3. Slide your injured arm gently into the other strap, without gripping or pulling anything. If that hurts, stop and ask your physio.
4. Fasten the hip belt first, so it carries most of the weight, then snug the shoulder straps and sternum strap with your uninjured hand. Check that nothing rubs your scar or presses on the injured arm.
5. To take it off, sit down, undo the hip belt, push the strap off your injured side's shoulder with your uninjured hand, then slip your uninjured arm out and lower the pack onto the bench.

The one-handed habits that go with this, for the commute, the changing room and the gym floor, are in [chapter 19](19-injury-prevention.md).

## If you have plates

### They usually stay in

For most adults, forearm plates stay for life. Once the bone has healed, they're compatible with normal training, carrying and hiking **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Removing them raises the re-fracture risk.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 23 studies found about 6 more re-fractures per 100 patients when forearm plates came out, and concluded that keeping them is linked to fewer re-fractures (Cao 2025). In another series, 12.9% of bones re-fractured after removal, against 2.8% with the plates kept in (Yao 2014) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Plate ends are stress risers.** The plate holds the bone stiff along its length, and the bone goes back to normal at its ends, so a hard fall can break the bone just beyond the end of a plate, even years later **[Expert opinion]**. That's one more reason to respect falls, and why question 7 asks your surgeon whether it means any long-term limits for you.
- **The ulna (the forearm bone on the little-finger side) sits just under the skin,** so a plate on it is the one you're most likely to feel **[Expert opinion]**. Mention any plate or screw that catches, aches or rubs at clinic rather than training through it. Thinning, red or broken skin over metalwork needs a same-day call.
- **Tell any future doctor, physio or radiographer that you have metalwork.**

### If removal is ever suggested

Plates usually come out only for a reason, such as pain, prominence or infection. Afterwards the bone is weaker for months: in older series, re-fractures came 2–40 weeks after removal (Hidaka 1984), and six of seven were in people who'd broken both bones (Deluca 1988) **[Moderate evidence]**. If it happens, remove your higher gate dates on the day of surgery, re-record each one only when your surgeon clears it, and treat falls and impact as the main risk for 4–6 months unless they say otherwise **[Expert opinion]**.

## Training your good arm: cross-education

Training one limb makes the opposite limb a little stronger, mostly through the nervous system. With one wrist in a cast for 3 weeks, training the free hand kept the cast arm's strength steady, while it fell without training (Farthing 2009). After a broken wrist, home training of the uninjured hand gave better grip at 12 weeks but not at 26 (Magnus 2013), and pooled reviews put the average gain at about 12% (Manca 2017), less clearly in patients with musculoskeletal conditions (Valdés 2026) **[Moderate evidence]**.

**Bottom line:** it probably helps a little, it's low-risk and it builds your other arm, but it doesn't replace physio. From Good arm OK (or any higher gate, Light use / rehab started included) the hub adds seated one-arm machine, cable and grip work on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays: stop 2–3 reps short of failure, breathe out on the effort, and keep the injured arm resting.

## Nerves, the scar and CRPS

- **Nerves.** Several run close to the forearm bones, and the injury or surgery can bruise or stretch them. When you report a symptom, say what it is (numb, tingling, burning or weak), where (which fingers, palm side or back of the hand) and when. Nerves regrow at about 1 mm a day, so recovery can take two years or more (BSSH). New or worsening symptoms are a red flag: tick the numbness and tingling flag in your check-in.
- **The scar,** if you have one. Follow your surgical team first. The NHS's general advice: massage only once the wound has fully healed, with a water-based cream, a few times a day for up to 10 minutes. Silicone gels or dressings can help. Protect it from the sun for at least a year (clothing or SPF 30+), and expect it to keep fading for 2 years or more.
- **CRPS (complex regional pain syndrome)** is uncommon. It's persistent pain out of proportion to the injury, often burning, sometimes with skin that's very sensitive to touch, swelling, stiffness, colour and sweating changes that come and go, or unusual hair or nail growth (NHS). Contact your fracture clinic or GP within days, because early treatment may reduce symptoms, and tick the colour and temperature red flag.

## Confidence and fear of re-injury

Guarding the arm is sensible now, but later it can hold you back. In wrist and hand injuries, anxiety, catastrophising and fear of movement are linked to worse outcomes (Minnucci 2026) **[Moderate evidence]**. The antidote is structure: the gates, red flags and pain rule give you a clear yes or no every day, each physio step is a small, safe dose of confidence, and leg strength and supported balance work are fall-proofing you can build now ([chapter 19](19-injury-prevention.md)). If worry or low mood is affecting your sleep, work or training, tell your GP or physio.

## Medicines, smoking and fuel for healing

- **Anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs),** such as ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac tablets or gel, and aspirin (NHS), have a debated effect on bone healing, and much of the evidence is low quality. One meta-analysis found higher odds of delayed union or non-union (odds ratio 2.07), but not for low-dose, short courses (Wheatley 2019). A 2024 meta-analysis found no significant link (Chuang 2024) **[Moderate evidence]**. **Ask the fracture clinic before taking them regularly,** including for training soreness.
- **Smoking.** Smokers had more than twice the odds of non-union in a systematic review (adjusted odds ratio 2.32; Scolaro 2014) **[Moderate evidence]**. If you smoke, stopping now still helps the bone. Ask your GP or pharmacist about NHS stop-smoking support.
- **Fuel the repair.** Don't diet. Hit your calorie, protein (about 1.8 g per kg of body weight a day, so about 145 g for an 80 kg adult), calcium and vitamin D targets ([chapter 14](14-nutrition.md)), and protect your sleep ([chapter 17](17-sleep-and-recovery.md)).
