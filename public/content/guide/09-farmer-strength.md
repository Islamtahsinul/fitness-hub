# Farmer Strength — Carries & Grip

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - **Right now, nothing is gripped, held, carried or pushed with your injured arm**, and there are no training carries in your uninjured hand either; everyday carrying follows [the one carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule). Farmer strength starts in your legs, hips and trunk, and that work is already in your plan.
> - Grip comes in three main types: **crush** (closing the hand), **support** (holding on) and **pinch** (thumb against fingers), plus wrist and forearm strength. Carries train grip, posture, core and hips together.
> - Progress follows the **grip ladder**, your injured hand as a percentage of your uninjured hand. At **70%** (with Grip cleared) light holds and carries begin; at **80%**, moderate farmer's carries; at **90%** (plus full clearance), heavy carries and hanging. Every rung needs a reading your physio took, or their OK.
> - Every stage also follows the **arm pain rule**: your physio's limit, or 2/10 or below during and back to your usual level by the next morning.
> - Long-term benchmarks: a quarter of your body weight in each hand for 60 s (20 kg a hand for the 80 kg example profile), later half your body weight per hand for 30–50 m, and a 60-s dead hang (the standards table below). Strongman ideas (Josh Bryant, Tom Haviland) come last.

"Farmer strength" is the strength to pick up something heavy and awkward and carry it a long way without fuss: shopping, a loaded rucksack, a bag of compost, a child, a water container to a campsite. It's grip, yes, but also upright posture, a stiff trunk, strong hips and the legs to walk under load. You can build most of that before your hand is allowed to hold anything.

---

## Why it matters

- **It's what real life asks for.** Peter Attia's examples of abilities worth keeping into old age include lifting a 30-lb (about 14 kg) suitcase into an overhead bin and climbing three flights of stairs with 10 lb (about 4.5 kg) of shopping in each hand ([Attia, 2022](https://peterattiamd.com/how-to-train-for-the-centenarian-olympics/)) **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Grip is a health marker.** In a study of nearly 140,000 adults in 17 countries, every 5 kg lower grip strength was associated with about a 16% higher risk of death from any cause and 17% higher risk of cardiovascular death. Grip predicted these more strongly than systolic blood pressure did ([Leong et al., 2015](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22prognostic%20value%20of%20grip%20strength%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22grip%20strength%20across%20the%20life%20course%3A%20normative%20data%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Strong evidence]** for the association. That's a marker, not proof that grip training itself extends life.
- **UK reference points.** Grip peaks in early adulthood at a median of about **51 kg for men** (ages 29–39) and **31 kg for women** (ages 26–42), then declines from midlife ([Dodds et al., 2014](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22prognostic%20value%20of%20grip%20strength%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22grip%20strength%20across%20the%20life%20course%3A%20normative%20data%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Strong evidence]**. Once Good arm OK is set, you'll log your uninjured hand's grip on the **Your arm** page. That number becomes the top of your injured hand's ladder. In your twenties and thirties your grip is usually close to that peak already.

> [!SAFETY]
> Until your physio clears gripping (Grip cleared), your injured hand holds **nothing**: no dumbbells, no bags, no shopping, no walking poles, no pulling gym doors with it. Until Good arm OK, don't train or test your uninjured hand either. If you notice increasing pain, new swelling, numbness or tingling, colour change, or anything that feels like it has given way, stop and contact the fracture clinic or NHS 111 ([Your Arm](18-arm-recovery.md)).

## The grip ladder and the arm pain rule

These two rules decide every step in this chapter. They're the same everywhere in the hub and guide.

| Injured-hand grip (% of your uninjured hand) | What it allows | Also needs |
|---|---|---|
| Below 70% | Your physio's programme only | — |
| At least 70% | Light holds and carries: suitcase carries, light farmer's carries, plate pinches, walking poles | Grip cleared, and your physio's reading or OK |
| At least 80% | Moderate farmer's carries | Grip cleared, and your physio's reading or OK |
| At least 90% | Heavy carries and hanging: heavy farmer's carries, sandbag carries, feet-supported hangs, dead hangs, pull-ups | Full clearance, and your physio's reading or OK |

A rung counts only when your physio has measured your grip, or OK'd the step: log the readings on the **Your arm** page. Most people's dominant hand is a little stronger than the other, so if the injured hand is your weaker one, the 90% rung means close to a full recovery; if it's your stronger one, a little more may come back after that ([Your Arm](18-arm-recovery.md) explains the numbers).

**Arm pain rule:** follow your physio's limit. If you weren't given one, keep pain at 2/10 or below during the exercise, back to your usual level by the next morning. Never train through pain that's increasing. If a carry breaks the rule, go back to the last weight that didn't for at least a week.

### The carrying rule, briefly

One rule covers every bag, pack and carry; its home is [the one carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule).
- **Training carries** (suitcase, farmer's, sandbag): none until Grip cleared, then the grip ladder above.
- **Daily life until Light use / rehab started:** nothing in your injured hand or on that side's shoulder. A light bag (3 kg or less) on the shoulder of your uninjured side, or a waist pack, is fine; put it on and take it off with your uninjured hand. On walks, a waist pack only (2 kg or less).
- **From Light use / rehab started:** a light day pack on both shoulders, 2–4 kg to start, hip belt fastened, put on seated, uninjured arm first; add about 1 kg every 2–3 weeks if the walk and the next morning feel fine.

## The anatomy of grip

Grip isn't one quality. Coaches usually split it into a few types, each trained a little differently **[Expert opinion]**.

| Type | What it is | Everyday example | How you'll train it | When (injured hand) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Crush** | Closing the hand hard | A firm handshake, wringing a cloth | Your physio's programme; gripper squeezes with your uninjured hand; grip readings | Physio first; loaded from Grip cleared |
| **Support** | Holding on for time | Carrying bags, hanging from a bar, a deadlift | Suitcase and farmer's carries, RDLs and deadlifts; later hangs | Carries from 70%; hangs from 90% plus full clearance |
| **Pinch** | Thumb against fingers | Carrying a plate, a board or a sandbag by its fabric | Plate pinch; later sandbag work | Plate pinch from 70% (Phase 3); sandbags at full clearance |
| **Wrist and forearm** | Bending, side-to-side and rotating (palm up and down) | Turning a key or door handle, planting a walking pole | Your physio's programme; wrist curls with your uninjured hand; hammer curls | Physio first |

Forearm rotation (pronation and supination) deserves special attention after a forearm fracture. It's often the slowest movement to come back, and your physio will measure it. Don't force it in the gym. It belongs to their programme.

## Carry variations

| Carry | How | Main training effect | Needs | In your plan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Suitcase carry** | One weight in one hand | Resisting side-bending (lateral core), grip, standing tall | Grip cleared, 70% and physio OK | Phase 2 onwards |
| **Farmer's carry** | A weight in each hand | Posture, upper back, grip endurance, breathing under load | Light from 70%, moderate from 80%, heavy from 90% plus full clearance | Phase 3 onwards; heavy in Phases 4–5 |
| **Sandbag bear-hug carry** | Hugging a sandbag to your chest | Front core, upper back, breathing while compressed; a forgiving grip | Full clearance | Light in Phase 4a, then circuits |
| **Sandbag shoulder carry** | A sandbag on one shoulder | Asymmetric trunk control; carrying awkward real-world loads | Full clearance | Phase 5 |
| **Loaded pack (rucking)** | A rucksack | Everything, for hours; hiking-specific | Light use / rehab started (light) → Full clearance (loaded) | Light from Light use / rehab started (often in Phase 1); loaded from Phase 4 |
| **Zercher carry, yoke, axle or thick-handle holds** | Strongman tools | Heavy, whole-body bracing | Full clearance plus a year of base | Not in the hub plan; later, with coaching |

The good-hand suitcase carry in the exercise library is gated to grip clearance, like every training carry, and even then it's only for when your physio suggests it. Walking with a weight in your uninjured hand while your injured arm can't catch you is a fall risk you don't need, so the good-arm block stays seated instead.

Stuart McGill's lab compared strongman events, including the farmer's walk and suitcase carry. Carrying events "challenged different abilities than the lifting events", and the authors suggested loaded carries could enhance a lifting-based programme ([McGill et al., 2009](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22strongman%20events%22%20AND%20AUTH:%22McGill%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. That's why carries sit alongside your squats and hinges, not instead of them.

## The staged path to farmer strength

Every stage has an **entry gate** (your clearance) and **entry criteria** (your rung on the grip ladder and your arm's response). Your physio's advice overrides all of it.

| Stage | When | What you do | Entry criteria | How you progress |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Foundation** | Now (Phases 0–1) | Legs, hips and trunk: the dead-start leg press, single-leg hip thrusts, single-leg work, the arm-free circuit, dead bugs | — | Double progression on every leg exercise |
| **1. Uninjured hand and cross-education** | Good arm OK (Phases 0–1) | Seated good-arm block on Mon/Wed/Fri: one-arm press and row, curls, then wrist curls and grip squeezes, 3 sets each at 2–3 reps in reserve | Clinic or physio says you may train your uninjured arm | Add reps, then load |
| **2. Rehab** | Light use / rehab started | Your physio's programme exactly as prescribed, logged on the **Your arm** page | Physio has started rehab | Exactly as your physio progresses it |
| **3. Machines, then light holds** | Grip cleared (Phase 2a, then Phase 2) | Two weeks of machines and cables only; then suitcase carries 4 × 30 s per side with the **same weight in each hand**, starting at 6–10 kg | Phase 2a done; injured hand **at least 70%** of your uninjured hand, measured or OK'd by your physio | Add 5–10 s, then 2 kg, when 4 × 30 s is easy and your arm is quiet the next morning |
| **4. Farmer's carry** | Grip cleared (Phase 3) | Light farmer's carries (about 10% of body weight per hand: 8 kg for the 80 kg example profile) and plate pinch 3 × 20 s per hand from 70%; moderate carries (towards 15% per hand, about 12 kg) from **80%** | Suitcase carries 4 × 30 s with 10 kg or more, within the pain rule; each rung measured or OK'd by your physio | Add 2 kg per hand when 4 × 30 s is easy |
| **5. Hanging, sandbags and heavy carries** | Full clearance (Phase 4a, then Phases 4–5) | Phase 4a: feet-supported hangs, then short dead hangs; light sandbag bear-hug carries. Phase 4: heavy farmer's carries building to a quarter of body weight per hand for 40–60 s, then towards half per hand; sandbag to shoulder and shoulder carries; pull-ups | Full clearance; injured hand **at least 90%**, measured or OK'd by your physio; no next-day pain from Stage 4 loads | Time first, then load; one heavy carry day and one circuit day a week |
| **6. Strongman flavour (optional)** | 12+ months in, full clearance | Thick-handle or axle holds without straps, heavier odd objects, Zercher carries, a yoke if your gym has one | Everything above solid and pain-free; ideally a coach watching | Small jumps, low reps, lots of rest |

## Realistic expectations after a forearm fracture

This section describes other people's results, not yours. Your fracture clinic is the authority on your arm.

- In series of adults with forearm shaft fractures treated with plates, the bones typically united in **about 13–14 weeks** ([Polat & Toy, 2022; Karatas et al., 2025](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22forearm%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22diaphyseal%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22both-bone%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22shaft%22%29%29%20AND%20%22grip%20strength%22%20AND%20adult&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8)) **[Moderate evidence]**. Open fractures, complications and individual healing can make it much longer.
- In one cohort, grip in the injured hand was still about **18% lower** than the uninjured side at follow-up, and forearm rotation was reduced ([Sahoo et al., 2023](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22forearm%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22diaphyseal%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22both-bone%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22shaft%22%29%29%20AND%20%22grip%20strength%22%20AND%20adult&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8)) **[Moderate evidence]**. In ladder terms, that's around the 80% rung.
- In a review of 944 patients, about a quarter had some kind of adverse event; nerve injury and the bone failing to unite were the most common. That's one reason clinics are cautious about loading ([Vasara et al., 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22forearm%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22diaphyseal%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22both-bone%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22shaft%22%29%29%20AND%20%22grip%20strength%22%20AND%20adult&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8)) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- Good-hand training helped grip in the fractured hand at 12 weeks after a wrist fracture, though the difference had faded by 26 weeks ([Magnus et al., 2013](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:23529145%20OR%20EXT_ID:19150859%20OR%20EXT_ID:20966196%20OR%20EXT_ID:22429184&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**.

**What that means for you:** expect your injured hand's grip to lag behind your legs and your other hand for months. Climb the ladder one rung at a time, and don't chase heavy carries early. Healing bone, tendons and scar tissue adapt more slowly than muscle.

## How to carry well

1. **Set up.** Weights beside your feet, not in front. Handles centred, so the weight doesn't tip forwards or back.
2. **Pick up like a deadlift.** Hips back, flat back, chest proud, then stand tall. Never lift with a rounded back or a twist.
3. **Stand tall.** Imagine a string lifting the top of your head. Ribs down, shoulders down and slightly back, eyes forwards.
4. **Grip firmly,** wrist straight. Use chalk if your gym allows. **Not straps**: they shift the load onto the wrist, which is exactly what your healing forearm doesn't need.
5. **Brace and breathe.** A moderate brace, short steady breaths. Never a breath-hold before full clearance.
6. **Walk smart.** Short, quick steps, rolling heel to toe, weights quiet at your sides, no swinging.
7. **Turn carefully.** Make wide, slow turns, or put the weights down and turn.
8. **Set down under control.** Hips back and knees bent, weights down beside your feet. Never drop them.

**Carry-specific cues:**
- **Suitcase:** shoulders level, don't lean away from the weight.
- **Bear-hug sandbag:** hug it high against your chest, elbows tucked; lift it from a bench at first.
- **Shoulder sandbag:** stay tall under it; swap shoulders every set.

## Programming carries

- **Placement:** carries go near the end of a session, after the main lifts, so a tired grip never compromises a heavy squat or hinge **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Dose:** 3–5 carries of 20–40 s (or 20–40 m), on 1–2 days a week. In Phase 3 that means Wednesday's farmer's carry and lighter suitcase carries in Friday's circuit.
- **Progress time first, then load:** add 5–10 s, then 2 kg per hand. Hold for a week if the arm grumbles.
- **Same weight both sides** until you reach the 90% rung.
- **Deload weeks:** the hub halves the sets; keep the same weights.
- **Log it:** seconds (or metres) and kg per hand in your session, plus a grip reading every 4 weeks or so on the **Your arm** page (taken by your physio, or with their OK) so you know your rung.

## Benchmarks and standards

| Level | Farmer's carry | Suitcase carry (each side) | Dead hang | Grip ladder |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Starting out (Phase 3) | 4 × 30 s at about 10% of body weight per hand (8 kg), building towards 15% (12 kg) | 4 × 30 s with 10–15 kg | Not yet | 70–80% |
| Solid (Phase 4) | A quarter of body weight per hand (20 kg) for 60 s | A quarter of body weight for 30 m | 60 s | 90% or more |
| Strong (Phase 5 and beyond) | Half your body weight per hand (40 kg each, your body weight in total) for 30–50 m | About 40% of body weight for 20 m | 90–120 s | About equal |

**This table is the farmer's-carry standard for the whole hub:** the Phase 3–5 targets in [The Programme](04-the-program.md) and the farmer's-carry test on Progress → Tests point here. These are coaching benchmarks **[Expert opinion]**, not research norms; the kilograms are for the 80 kg example profile: work out yours from your own body weight, and they rise as you gain. The "strong" farmer's standard is a long-term goal. Most people take well over a year to reach it, and that's fine. The ladder column is your green light.

## Pavel, Josh Bryant, Tom Haviland and strongman

**Pavel Tsatsouline (StrongFirst).** The StrongFirst approach builds grip mainly as a by-product of practising heavy, crisp hinges, swings and hangs, never to failure, often with grease-the-groove practice spread through the week **[Expert opinion]**. One StrongFirst instructor greased the groove on pull-ups with sets of about 30–40% of his maximum reps, at least 45 minutes apart. He also stresses that connective tissue adapts more slowly than muscle, so loading should rise gradually ([Moseley, StrongFirst](https://www.strongfirst.com/the-three-pillars-for-healthy-aging-meet-the-strongfirst-standards/)) **[Expert opinion]**. For you, grip GTG (short, easy dead hangs) waits for full clearance and the 90% rung.

**Josh Bryant and Tom Haviland.** Their "Physically Fortified – Strength" programme puts the **farmer's walk** among four foundational lifts, alongside the Zercher squat, seal row and overhead press, progressed hard and tested at the end of the cycle. It uses double-overhand axle holds without straps, with the line "Grip is the mission". It treats neck, forearms, grip and calves as "the armor most lifters skip", though it allows straps when the goal is pure muscle growth ([JoshStrength, 2026](https://joshstrength.com/2026/03/physically-fortified-strength-program/)) **[Expert opinion]**. What to take from it now is the **armour idea**: your tibialis raises and calf work are armour you can build today. Axle holds and Zerchers wait for full clearance and a year of base.

**Strongman.** Carries, sandbags and odd objects build a kind of strength that barbells alone don't, as the McGill study above suggests. Strongman tools (yokes, heavy Zercher carries, stones) bring high spine loads and awkward positions ([McGill et al., 2009](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22strongman%20events%22%20AND%20AUTH:%22McGill%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. They're a "someday, with a coach" option, not part of this plan.

## Your grip-training menu, by gate

| Gate (and rung) | Uninjured hand | Injured hand | Both hands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Now | Nothing | Nothing | Legs, hips and trunk: the foundation |
| Good arm OK | Grip readings (Your arm page); seated grip squeezes, wrist curls, curls, one-arm press and row | Nothing | — |
| Light use / rehab started | As above | Your physio's programme only | — |
| Grip cleared, below 70% | — | — | Machines and cables (Phase 2a), RDLs and rows within the pain rule; no holds or carries |
| Grip cleared, 70% (physio's reading or OK) | — | — | Suitcase carries, light farmer's carries, plate pinch, walking poles |
| Grip cleared, 80% (physio's reading or OK) | — | — | Moderate farmer's carries |
| Full clearance, 90% (physio's reading or OK) | — | — | Feet-supported then full dead hangs, pull-ups, heavy farmer's carries, sandbags, swings and get-ups, deadlifts |

## Common mistakes and fixes

- **"Just carrying the shopping" with your injured hand before clearance.** Fix: a light bag (3 kg or less) on the shoulder of your uninjured side, a waist pack or a trolley, as [the one carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule) sets out. Everyday carrying counts as loading.
- **Skipping grip readings.** Fix: agree with your physio how both hands get measured every 4 weeks or so, and log them on the **Your arm** page. Without them you don't know your rung.
- **Going heavy because your uninjured hand can.** Fix: your injured hand sets the weight until you reach 90%.
- **Using straps to "protect" the arm.** Fix: no straps. They load the wrist. Lighten the weight instead.
- **Leaning and shuffling.** Fix: lighter load, taller posture, quicker steps.
- **Dropping the weights at the end.** Fix: set them down under control. Dropped dumbbells and a jolted wrist don't mix.

## Your actions this week

- **Train the foundation hard:** every leg, hip and core session in Re-entry Week as written. That's farmer strength, stage 0.
- **At your next fracture-clinic appointment,** use the questions on the **Your arm** page, and record any clearance the same day.
- **Once Good arm OK is set, log your uninjured hand's grip** on the **Your arm** page with a hand dynamometer (a basic digital one is about £15–25), or ask your physio to record it. It's the top of your ladder.
- **Change your everyday carrying to fit the carrying rule:** a light bag (3 kg or less) on the shoulder of your uninjured side or a waist pack, a trolley, two lighter trips instead of one heavy one.
- **Check what your gym has:** chalk, sandbags, a pull-up bar you can reach from a step, a thick-handle attachment. Note it for later phases.
