# Supplements — What Works, What Doesn't

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - Food, training and sleep do almost all the work. Supplements add the last few per cent, and only two are worth your money right now.
> - **Take vitamin D, 10 µg a day** from October to March (under £1 a month for tablets). **Creatine monohydrate powder, 3–5 g a day** (roughly £2–7 a month) is **optional**: fine to start now, unless you have any kidney concerns or take regular medication, in which case ask your GP first. This chapter is the one place that decides it.
> - **If you eat halal or vegetarian:** creatine powder is made from chemicals, not animals. Choose vitamin D **tablets** or a vegan D3 rather than gelatine softgels, and, if you buy a protein powder, **halal-certified** whey (if you eat halal) or a pea or soya powder.
> - **Optional:** whey on days you can't eat enough (£25–40 a month for a daily scoop), and a home-made coffee before training. **Skip** everything on the skip list and spend the money on food.
> - **Safety first:** show your GP, pharmacist or fracture clinic everything you take. No supplement is guaranteed clean.

## Safety, halal and vegetarian checks before you buy anything

- **Medicines change the picture.** The BDA warns that supplements can interact with medicines, "such as stopping blood thinners", and names antibiotics too. If you take any regular medicine, ask your pharmacist or GP before adding a supplement. After a fracture, especially an open one or after surgery, you may have had long courses of antibiotics. Some minerals (calcium, iron, zinc, magnesium) can stop certain antibiotics being absorbed, so ask your pharmacist about spacing them. **[Expert opinion]**
- **More is not better.** "Follow the doses that are stated on the label" (BDA).
- **Contamination is real.** UK Anti-Doping says "there are no guarantees that any supplement product is free from banned substances". Single-ingredient products such as plain creatine, vitamin D tablets and plain whey from established UK retailers are the lowest-risk choices. Batch-tested (Informed Sport) products cost more and matter most if you're ever drug-tested in sport. **[Expert opinion]**
- **Capsules and softgels are usually gelatine**, often from pork or beef. If you avoid gelatine, prefer powders and tablets, or look for "vegan", "vegetarian capsule" or a halal logo.
- **Avoid "proprietary blends"** that hide the dose of each ingredient. **[Expert opinion]**
- **Add one thing at a time**, so you know what's doing what. **[Expert opinion]**
- **Take a list to your next fracture-clinic appointment**, with every supplement and medicine you use.

## Creatine monohydrate: optional, and worth it

### When to start

This is the guide's only rule on starting creatine; other chapters point here.

- **It's optional.** Nothing in your programme depends on it. It helps a little, over months.
- **Ask your GP first if** you have any kidney concerns (a past kidney problem, or a blood or urine test that flagged your kidneys) or you take any regular medication, prescribed or bought, including regular painkillers or a long course of antibiotics.
- **Otherwise it's fine to start now,** at 3–5 g a day. You don't need to wait for the fracture clinic. Put it on the supplement list you take to your next fracture-clinic appointment anyway, and tell any doctor you take it before a blood test.
- **Took it before the injury?** Then you already know how you respond. Restart at 3–5 g a day, with no loading, once the rule above says yes.

### What it does and how to take it

**What it does.** More phosphocreatine means a little more quality work in each hard set, and over months that adds up to more muscle and strength. The ISSN calls creatine monohydrate "the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available to athletes" for high-intensity exercise and lean mass. **[Strong evidence]**

**Dose.** Take **3–5 g a day**, every day, including rest days. You don't need to "load": in one study, 3 g a day for 28 days filled the muscles about as much as 20 g a day for 6 days, so allow **at least 4 weeks** to feel the full effect.

**Timing.** Any time of day; Galpin says it "can be taken any time of the day". Stir it into your breakfast porridge or overnight oats so you never forget, and tick it in your check-in. During a fast, take it (and your vitamin D) with a meal in your eating window (suhoor or iftar, in Ramadan).

**Halal, vegetarian and budget.** Creatine monohydrate is made in a factory from simple chemicals: one manufacturing route described in a review combines sodium sarcosinate and cyanamide. It isn't made from meat, so plain powder suits halal and vegetarian diets. Buy **powder, not capsules or tablets**: tablets cost more per gram, and capsule shells may be gelatine. Plain monohydrate is the most studied form, so fancy versions aren't worth extra (ISSN).

**What to expect.** You may gain up to about 1 kg early on, as water moves into your muscles. The ISSN describes short-term fluid retention of about 0.5–1.0 L with loading, and over longer periods studies show no change in body water relative to muscle (Antonio and colleagues). It isn't fat.

**Safety.** The ISSN position stand found no compelling evidence of harm at doses up to 30 g a day for 5 years. A review of common misconceptions concluded that creatine "does not result in kidney damage and/or renal dysfunction in healthy individuals", found no human study reporting hair loss, and found no evidence that it causes dehydration or cramps. **[Strong evidence]**

**Your arm.** The evidence is mixed but encouraging. **[Moderate evidence]**

- During **upper-arm immobilisation in a cast**, short-term creatine reduced the loss of muscle and strength (Johnston and colleagues, 2009).
- During **leg immobilisation**, creatine loading didn't prevent losses (Backx and colleagues, 2017).
- During **rehabilitation** after disuse, creatine boosted muscle regrowth with strength training (Hespel and colleagues, 2001).

So it may help your injured arm regain muscle once rehab starts, and the rest of you as you retrain. It costs little and has no known downside for healthy people. It isn't a fracture-healing supplement.

**Cautions.** Creatine can raise creatinine on a blood test, which can look like a kidney problem, so tell the doctor you take it before any blood test. **[Expert opinion]** If you develop a kidney problem or start a regular medicine, pause it and go back to "When to start".

> [!TIP]
> **One-handed creatine.** Buy a tub with a flip-top lid, or pre-portion a week of doses into a pill organiser on Sunday.

## Vitamin D: take it from October to March

- **Dose:** 10 µg (400 IU) a day from October to March. The NHS advises considering it all year if you have dark skin (for example, an African, African-Caribbean or South Asian background) or you're rarely outdoors. **[Strong evidence]** as UK public-health advice.
- **Upper limit:** don't take more than 100 µg (4,000 IU) a day unless a doctor prescribes it (NHS).
- **Healing:** extra vitamin D hasn't clearly sped up fracture healing, and in a small wrist-fracture trial the higher dose "may be detrimental". **[Moderate evidence]** [Chapter 14](14-nutrition.md) has the details. Stick to 10 µg unless your GP tests you and advises more.
- **Gelatine-free forms (halal-friendly and vegetarian):**
  - **Tablets** are the cheapest choice and usually contain no gelatine. Check the ingredients, or look for "suitable for vegetarians".
  - **Vegan D3** costs a little more and rules out animal sources. The NHS tells vegans to "read the label to ensure the vitamin D used in a product is not of animal origin".
  - **Softgels** are usually gelatine. If you avoid gelatine, choose them only if they're labelled vegan or vegetarian, or halal-certified if you eat halal.
- **Cost:** own-brand 10 µg tablets were £2.75 for 90 or £4 for 180 when checked in November 2026, well under £1 a month. Many shops sell 25 µg tablets; that's still well under the upper limit, but there's no need to pay more for a higher dose.

## Protein powder: food, not magic

**When it helps:** on days you can't reach your protein target from food, or when a shake is easier than cooking one-handed.

**The evidence:** across many trials, protein supplements added an average of **0.30 kg** of extra lean mass to resistance training. They work mainly by raising total protein, and the benefit levels off above about **1.6 g/kg a day** (128 g for the 80 kg example profile) (Morton and colleagues). **[Strong evidence]** The meal templates already give 131–152 g, so on most days a powder adds nothing.

**If you eat halal: certified whey.** Whey is a by-product of cheese-making, so it raises the same rennet question as cheese, plus flavourings and other additives. A halal logo with the certifier's name answers those questions for you. If a product page doesn't say, ask the seller. Pea or soya powders are plant-only, so the rennet question doesn't arise, but check the flavourings.

**Cost:** at supermarket prices (checked in November 2026), whey works out at about **80p–£1.20 per 20 g of protein**, or roughly £25–40 a month for a scoop every day. Milk, own-brand yoghurt, tinned tuna and soya mince are all cheaper ([chapter 15](15-meal-plans.md)). If you buy whey, buy a big tub on offer.

**Choosing one:** concentrate is cheapest; isolate is worth the extra only if lactose bothers you. Aim for 20–40 g of protein per serving (ISSN).

**Timing:** no need to panic about an "anabolic window". The ISSN says timing is largely down to personal preference, because a session's effect lasts at least 24 hours. **[Strong evidence]**

## Caffeine: timing it for an early session

**Does it work?** Yes. The ISSN says caffeine "has consistently been shown to improve exercise performance" at doses of 3–6 mg/kg, and doses as low as about 2 mg/kg may work. **[Strong evidence]**

**How much is safe?** The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) says single doses up to **200 mg** and up to **400 mg a day** don't raise safety concerns for healthy adults.

**Your dose:** about **2–3 mg/kg**: roughly 160–240 mg for the 80 kg example profile, or one or two coffees. Keep any single dose to 200 mg or less (the EFSA level above), so if you're heavier you don't need the top of the range. Coffee strength varies hugely, so judge by how you feel, not the cup. A coffee or tea at home costs pennies; you don't need a pre-workout product.

**Your timing:** for a 06:00 start, a coffee when you wake at 05:15–05:30 gives you 30–45 minutes, which is plenty in practice. **[Expert opinion]** Have your **last caffeine about 8 hours before bed** (by 13:00–13:30 for a 21:15 lights out); [chapter 17](17-sleep-and-recovery.md) explains why. Skip caffeine if it makes you anxious or jittery, and never use it to cover a short night.

## Omega-3: eat the fish instead

- **Food:** the NHS recommends at least **2 portions of fish a week, including 1 oily**. A tin of sardines (about 60–75p) does that job, with calcium thrown in.
- **Capsules for muscle and soreness:** a meta-analysis found "low-quality evidence" that omega-3 supplements don't give a clinically important reduction in muscle soreness. A 2025 review found no effect on muscle protein synthesis rates, and small muscle benefits appear mainly in older adults. So there's **[Moderate evidence]** that capsules add little for you if you eat oily fish.
- **If you don't eat fish,** or stop, an algae-oil capsule with a vegetarian shell is the vegetarian and halal-friendly option. **[Expert opinion]**
- **Avoid fish-liver oils** such as cod liver oil, which contain vitamin A (NHS).

## Gelatine or collagen with vitamin C: later, and optional

**The idea:** tendons and ligaments are made mostly of collagen. Keith Baar's lab tested whether taking gelatine (rich in glycine and proline) plus vitamin C shortly before loading the tissue helps build more collagen.

**The key study (Shaw and colleagues, 2017):** eight young men took 15 g of gelatine with 48 mg of vitamin C, **1 hour before 6 minutes of rope skipping**. A blood marker of collagen-making rose more than with placebo. The limits are big: only eight people, a short study, and a marker that probably reflects bone collagen.

**Since then:** a 2026 systematic review concluded that 15–30 g of collagen with at least 50 mg of vitamin C "may enhance tendon remodeling", while finding strong evidence of **no** effect on muscle strength. There is no evidence that it speeds fracture healing. **[Expert opinion]** overall: promising, but unproven.

**For you:** this waits for the Light use / rehab started gate and your physio's agreement. If you try it then, use bovine or fish gelatine or collagen (**halal-certified** if you eat halal; there's no vegetarian version with the same evidence), about 15 g with about 50 mg of vitamin C (roughly 150 ml of orange juice), 30–60 minutes before a rehab session. It's an incomplete protein, so don't count it towards your target. Skip it if money is tight.

## Calcium, multivitamins and vitamin B12

- **Calcium:** the meal templates give 1,400–2,000 mg a day, twice the 700 mg need, so you don't need a supplement. The NHS says up to 1,500 mg a day from supplements is unlikely to cause harm, and the BDA suggests talking to a pharmacist or GP before adding more than 500 mg.
- **Multivitamins:** the NHS says most people "should get all the nutrients they need by having a varied and balanced diet". A basic one is optional insurance if your diet becomes limited. It won't build muscle or speed healing, so skip it and save the money. **[Expert opinion]**
- **Vitamin B12:** if you eat dairy, eggs, fish or meat, you don't need a supplement. If you're vegan, you need a reliable source, such as fortified foods or a supplement (NHS).

## Electrolytes for long or hot hikes

- This is about salt; carbohydrate on walks follows the one long-walk rule in [chapter 14](14-nutrition.md) (start eating at about 60 minutes).
- On hikes over about 2–3 hours, or on hot, sweaty days, add salt: salted peanuts, crisps, or a drink with about **200–400 mg of sodium** (Galpin). **[Moderate evidence]** The ACSM says drinks with electrolytes and carbohydrate "can provide benefits over water alone" during exercise.
- Salty snacks do the job of branded tablets for far less. You don't need electrolytes in a cool gym.

## Sleep supplements: fix the basics first

- Huberman suggests a sleep stack of magnesium, apigenin and theanine, and himself says to "start with one supplement (or none!)". The human evidence is thin, so it's **[Expert opinion]**. Light, timing and caffeine matter far more ([chapter 17](17-sleep-and-recovery.md)).
- **Melatonin is prescription-only in the UK** (NHS), so don't buy it online.

## What to skip to save money

| Product | Why to skip it | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Testosterone boosters** | In one analysis, 90% of products claimed to boost testosterone, but only 24.8% of those had any data behind the claim. 10.1% contained ingredients that may lower testosterone, and some exceeded safe upper limits for zinc, vitamin B3 or magnesium | **[Moderate evidence]** |
| **BCAAs and EAA drinks** | A review found no human studies that had measured the effect of BCAAs alone, and called the claim that they stimulate muscle protein synthesis unwarranted. Whole protein already contains them | **[Moderate evidence]** |
| **Mass gainers** | Mostly sugar plus some protein at a premium price. Your home-made milkshake gives about 530 kcal and 20 g of protein for roughly 55p ([chapter 15](15-meal-plans.md)) | **[Expert opinion]** |
| **Pre-workouts and fat burners** | You're eating for a surplus, not cutting. Big stimulant doses harm sleep, complex blends carry a higher contamination risk (UKAD), and a coffee does the job | **[Expert opinion]** |
| **"Bone-healing" or "joint" blends** | No good human evidence that they speed fracture healing. They stack calcium, vitamin D, vitamin K and zinc, so you can double up, and vitamin K interacts with blood thinners | **[Expert opinion]** |
| **Fish oil, multivitamins, sleep stacks and collagen "for joints"** | Little or no benefit for you on your current diet, and many come in gelatine capsules | **[Moderate evidence]** for fish oil, **[Expert opinion]** for the rest |
| **Protein bars and ready-to-drink shakes** | Convenient, but much dearer per gram of protein than milk or yoghurt, and some contain gelatine or collagen | **[Expert opinion]** |
| **High-dose vitamin A and fish-liver oils** | More than 1.5 mg a day over years may weaken bones (NHS) | **[Moderate evidence]** |
| **Megadoses of vitamin D** | Over 100 µg a day can raise blood calcium (NHS), and a higher dose may have hindered wrist-fracture healing | **[Moderate evidence]** |
