# The Engine — Zone 2, VO2max & Conditioning

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - Your numbers come from your age: **HRmax ≈ 208 − 0.7 × age**, **Zone 2 = 60–70% of HRmax**, and the MAF ceiling is **180 − age**, minus 10 while you're recovering from injury. For the example profile (a 30-year-old) that's **HRmax about 187 bpm**, **Zone 2 = 112–131 bpm** and **MAF 150 (140 while recovering)**. Settings shows your own figures. For weeks 1–4, stay in the lower half of Zone 2.
> - Build the **aerobic base** first and raise the **ceiling (VO2max)** second. About four of every five sessions are easy: full sentences possible, but you'd rather not talk.
> - **If you use a wearable, read Zone 2 as its live bpm number,** not its zone colours, and wear it on your uninjured arm. A chest strap is the accurate choice, and the talk test is free.
> - **Arm-safe now:** the stair climber and incline treadmill (uninjured hand lightly on the rail, injured arm across your stomach, safety key on, never stepping onto a moving belt) and the upright bike. Until Full clearance, hard intervals happen on the bike only.
> - **The rower and ski erg** need Grip cleared at the earliest, with your physio's OK, and steady work only. Hard intervals on them wait for Full clearance. Sled pushing waits for Full clearance.

## Why the engine matters for you

Your second goal, hiking long distances across all terrains, is an endurance sport. A six-hour hill day mostly tests how much energy your aerobic system can supply, hour after hour. Strength gets you up the steep steps, and the engine gets you through hour five.

It matters for the rest of your life too. In **122,007** adults followed for a median of 8.4 years after a treadmill test, the fittest group had about one-fifth of the risk of death of the least fit (adjusted hazard ratio 0.20), with "no observed upper limit of benefit" (Mandsager 2018). That's **[Strong evidence]** for the association, although it can't prove cause and effect. Peter Attia's site makes the same point: moving from the bottom 25% of VO2max to the next band up is associated with about a **50%** lower risk of death **[Moderate evidence]**. The UK baseline is at least **150 minutes** of moderate activity a week plus strength work on **2 days** **[Strong evidence]**. Your programme goes well beyond that.

## The two parts of the engine

**The base: Zone 2.** Attia defines it as "the highest metabolic output/work that you can sustain while keeping your lactate level below two millimole per liter". In plain English, it's the fastest pace your muscles can keep up mainly by burning fuel with oxygen. Training here builds more and better **mitochondria**, grows **capillaries**, expands **blood plasma volume** and, over months, enlarges the heart's main pumping chamber.

| When | What changes | What you notice |
|---|---|---|
| Days to 4 weeks | Plasma volume rises and capillaries grow | The same walk feels easier, and your HR is a few beats lower |
| 1–3 months | More mitochondria, better fat burning | Your Zone 2 pace gets faster, and you recover from long sessions more quickly |
| 3–12 months | Bigger stroke volume, lower resting HR | Hills feel easier, and long days stop draining you |

Uphill Athlete's rule is to give any sensible plan **six months** before judging it. In a year-long marathon study they summarise, the heart's main chamber didn't change much until about month six **[Moderate evidence]**. Their warning on aerobic deficiency, a strong top end on a weak base, is blunt: "Training harder will not speed this process." The signs are breathing that shoots up on slight uphills, not being able to talk during most endurance work, and needing to finish exhausted for a session to feel worthwhile **[Expert opinion]**. Years of lifting mean you already know how to train consistently, but lifting builds only part of the aerobic base a six-hour hill day needs, and an injury layoff costs some fitness. Expect the early changes in the table to come quickly; the deeper ones still take months **[Expert opinion]**.

**The ceiling: VO2max.** This is the maximum rate at which your body can take in and use oxygen. A higher ceiling means any hill uses a smaller share of your maximum. You raise it with short, hard efforts. The best-known protocol is the Norwegian **4 × 4**: four 4-minute bouts at 90–95% of HRmax, with 3 minutes of easier recovery between. In a randomised trial with 40 moderately trained men, doing it three times a week for 8 weeks improved VO2max more than steady training matched for total work (+7.2%) **[Moderate evidence]**. It was a small trial, so your exact gains will differ.

**The 80/20 split.** Stephen Seiler, on the Uphill Athlete podcast, describes the pattern as "about four of every five sessions" easy. Hilly terrain drags people into the middle zone without meaning to, so "too hard, too often" is the likeliest mistake for hikers **[Moderate evidence]**.

| Who | The big idea | What we take for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Peter Attia** | Zone 2 is the aerobic pillar, and Zone 5 (VO2max) the second | Most minutes in Zone 2, plus one short, hard session a week once the base is built |
| **Andy Galpin** (Huberman Lab) | Endurance is several qualities, and mechanics (breathing, posture, technique) come first | Start with long-duration and muscular endurance, which are safe now |
| **Andrew Huberman** (Foundational Fitness Protocol) | One long Zone 2 session a week, and 180–200 minutes of Zone 2 a week | Your Saturday long session and weekly Zone 2 target |
| **Uphill Athlete** | Base first, then intensity, then muscular endurance as a separate phase | Months of easy volume now, and harder hill work later ([chapter 11](11-hiking-training.md)) |

## Your zones

The hub calculates **HRmax with Tanaka's formula**, 208 − 0.7 × age, which comes from pooling 351 studies (18,712 people) and was checked in the lab on 514 more **[Strong evidence]**. For the example profile, a 30-year-old, that gives 208 − 21 = **187 bpm**. It's an average, and individuals vary around it, so treat the numbers as a starting point. Settings shows your own HRmax and zones once your birth year is set.

| Zone | % of HRmax | Example profile (HRmax 187) | How it feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| Z1 | 50–60% | 94–112 | Easy strolling, recovery |
| **Z2** | **60–70%** | **112–131** | Full sentences possible, but you'd rather not talk. Nasal breathing possible |
| Z3 | 70–80% | 131–150 | Comfortably hard, short sentences only |
| Z4 | 80–90% | 150–168 | Hard, a few words at a time |
| Z5 | 90–100% | 168–187 | Very hard, can't talk |

**The talk test, your referee.** A review found that comfortable speech is likely below the ventilatory or lactate threshold and unlikely to be above it, across walking, cycling and stair-stepping. A 2022 critique judged the talk test a valid marker of the first threshold **[Strong evidence]**. Uphill Athlete's coaches find it less precise in people without much endurance training, so pair it with your heart rate and use it as a daily check that easy really is easy **[Expert opinion]**. **How:** after 10 minutes at a steady pace, say a long sentence aloud, or count from 1 to 20. If you have to break it for breath, ease off. If you could sing, go a little harder.

**The MAF cross-check.** Phil Maffetone's formula starts at 180 − age, which is **150** for the example profile. While you're recovering from injury, Maffetone suggests −10, which gives **140**: the ceiling on easy days. Strictly, his −10 is for anyone recovering from an operation or a hospital stay, or in rehabilitation (an injury on its own gets −5). The hub's Settings page shows your own figures, in the form "MAF cross-check for Zone 2: 180 − age = 150 bpm. While recovering from injury, Maffetone suggests −10 → 140 bpm". The example's Zone 2 (112–131) sits well below that ceiling, which is expected: MAF is a ceiling, not a target **[Expert opinion]**.

**Putting it together:**
1. **Weeks 1–4:** stay in the lower half of Zone 2 (60–65% of HRmax, about 112–122 for the example profile). After weeks or months off, your tendons and feet are re-adapting too.
2. **Then:** use the whole Zone 2 band, and never go above your MAF ceiling on an easy day (about 140 for the example profile while you're recovering).
3. **If your monitor and your breathing disagree,** believe the talk test. HR runs high on hot, under-slept or caffeinated days, and monitors misread.
4. **Judge after 10 minutes.** HR lags behind effort by 3–5 minutes, and on climbs you let the pace drop to hold the zone.
5. **Uninjured hand lightly on the rail, never leaning.** Leaning flatters the numbers and puts weight through an arm. The injured arm rests across your stomach.

**The heart-rate drift test (optional, after about 8 weeks).** Once you can do 60 minutes comfortably on the incline treadmill, Uphill Athlete's test finds your personal aerobic threshold. Use a chest strap or an upper-arm sensor, because they say wrist sensors aren't accurate enough. Warm up for 10–15 minutes to the top of your current Zone 2, then walk 40–60 minutes at a **constant** speed at 10% incline, with your uninjured hand resting lightly on the rail and never leaning. Drift % = (second-half average HR ÷ first-half average − 1) × 100. Under 3.5% means start 5 bpm higher next time, 3.5–5% means your starting HR is your aerobic threshold, and over 5% means start lower **[Expert opinion]**. Note the result when you log the session. Only fill in **HRmax override** in Settings if you have a *measured* HRmax.

## Reading Zone 2 on a wearable

A wrist or arm wearable is a good tool for steady work if you wear and read it the right way.

- **Wear it on your uninjured arm, and never on the injured forearm.** If it's a strap you can move, the upper arm is often the better spot: in a 2025 study of 28 young adults, one screenless optical strap worn on the upper arm agreed best with a chest strap during warm-ups and treadmill work. The forearm position showed systematic bias, and every position struggled during burpees (Moghaddam 2025) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Read the number, not the colours.** Keep the live heart rate inside your own Zone 2 from Settings. A device's built-in zones may use a different maximum or method from the hub's, so its "Zone 2" label may not match yours.
- **Make the number visible.** If your device has no screen, show the live heart rate in its phone app if it can, and rest the phone on the machine's console at eye level, never in your hand.
- **Check it every 10 minutes,** and run the talk test at the same moment. If they disagree, the talk test wins.
- **For intervals, go by effort.** Optical sensors lag and drift as heart rate rises: a meta-analysis found wrist readings close to a chest strap on the treadmill, but further off in cycling and resistance training (Zhang 2020) **[Strong evidence]**.
- **Recovery or readiness scores are interesting, but the hub's readiness rule decides your day.** A low device score isn't an AMBER day on its own. Log your resting HR, sleep, soreness and energy, and follow the hub.

**Your resting heart rate.** Many wearables take resting HR from your sleep. In a study over 536 nights, one such strap's overnight readings agreed reasonably well with an ECG (Dial 2025) **[Moderate evidence]**. A reading taken in bed after waking is usually a few beats higher **[Expert opinion]**. The hub makes a day AMBER when your morning HR is at least 5 bpm above your 7-day average, so mixing methods could trigger a false AMBER. **Pick your long-term method now**, either a 60-second pulse count or a chest-strap reading in bed, and enter only that in the check-in, even if a wearable shows its own number. If you do switch later, press **I changed how I measure resting HR** in Settings on the day you switch. The hub then waits for 5 readings with the new method before the resting-HR rule counts again.

## Choosing a heart-rate set-up

- **The talk test is free,** works on every machine and on the hill, and stays your referee whatever device you buy.
- **A chest strap is the accurate choice.** It's the reference device in validation studies, it's the most responsive in intervals, and it's what the drift test needs. Most pair with free phone apps over Bluetooth (check your gym's machines before relying on them). Wet the electrodes before you put it on.
- **Fastening it needs two hands.** Until you've set Light use / rehab started, ask someone to clip it, or use an **upper-arm optical band** on your uninjured arm, which slides on one-handed. In the same 2025 study, an armband optical sensor (the Polar Verity Sense, as one example) agreed with the chest strap about as closely as the upper-arm strap did (Moghaddam 2025).
- **A budget wrist band** is fine for steady walking and resting-HR trends, but it's weaker for cycling and gym work (Zhang 2020).

Before you buy, [chapter 17](17-sleep-and-recovery.md) covers trackers, resting HR and HRV in more detail.

## How much, and how often

Your programme sets the exact minutes each week ([chapter 04](04-the-program.md)). This is the shape of it:

| Phase | Zone 2 per week | Long session | Hard session | Engine machines |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0, Re-entry Week (programme week 1) | about 90 min | 40-min walk | None | Stair climber 8 min and incline walk 24 min, twice |
| 1 (8 weeks, repeats until Grip cleared) | about 150 min | Walk, 45 → 80 min | Bike 30/90s (a Zone 3 primer first) | Stair climber 9 → 25 min, incline walk about 30 min |
| 2a–3 (Grip cleared) | 140–160 min | 80-min walk with a light pack | Bike 4 × 4, then 5 × 4 | In Phase 3, treadmill walking with your pack |
| 4a (Full clearance bridge) | about 140 min | 80 min, first rough terrain | Bike 5 × 4 | First steady ski erg |
| 4–5 | about 240 min | About 1¾ hours, building to 5–6 hours | Uphill 4 × 4, hill intervals | Steady ski erg or rower |

Your daily walks count on top, building towards **8,000–10,000 steps a day**. Brisk ones can take you to Huberman's 180–200 minutes a week during Phase 1.

Progression rules **[Expert opinion]**:
- Increase **one thing at a time**: minutes, incline or speed.
- Deload weeks drop the volume. The hub has already written the shorter minutes into those weeks.
- If your morning resting HR stays raised, your sleep falls apart, or the arm aches more, hold or drop back for a week.

## Intervals: raising the ceiling

**When:** Thursdays (Wednesdays from Phase 4). In Phase 1 that's a bike session of 30 seconds hard and 90 seconds easy, × 6. The first weeks are a primer at Zone 3 effort (6/10), and the hub's week notes tell you when the efforts become properly hard (8/10). Progress by raising the resistance, not the rounds. Phases 2–3 move to the 4 × 4, then the 5 × 4. Each 4-minute rep should end at about **85–95% of HRmax: 159–178 bpm for the example profile**. Never do intervals on an AMBER day. Ride them all in Zone 2 instead.

**Where:** the **upright bike only, until Full clearance**. Stay **seated**, because standing on the pedals means gripping the bars with both hands. Keep your uninjured hand light on the bar and the injured arm across your stomach or resting on your thigh. Hard efforts on the stair climber or treadmill are off the table for now, because tired legs on a moving belt or step are exactly the fall risk you're avoiding. After clearance, Phase 4 moves the 4 × 4 onto the stair climber and Phase 5 uses steep treadmill intervals.

- **Bookend every session** with easy minutes: the hub's session includes the warm-up and cool-down.
- **Use effort, not heart rate, for short efforts.** HR barely catches up in 30 seconds, and Attia's AMA on cardio training calls heart rate "not a reliable metric" for setting VO2max interval intensity **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Breathe the whole time.** No breath-holding or straining (Valsalva) before clearance.
- **Stop** if your form falls apart, the arm throbs, or you feel dizzy.

## Gym machines: what's arm-safe now and what comes later

Until Full clearance, every cardio machine follows one rule: uninjured hand lightly on the rail (a touch, not a lean), injured arm across your stomach, safety key clipped on, and never step onto a moving belt or stairs.

| Machine | Gate | How to use it, or why it waits |
|---|---|---|
| **Stair climber** | Now | Step on only when the stairs are stopped, then start it slowly. Uninjured hand lightly on the rail, injured arm across your stomach, safety clip on. To finish, slow to the lowest speed, press stop and wait until the stairs stop completely before stepping off. If you wobble, slow the machine before you grab anything |
| **Incline treadmill** | Now | Stand on the stopped belt, clip the safety key on, rest your uninjured hand lightly on the rail, then start the belt at its slowest speed and build up. Never step onto a belt that's already moving. Walk at 4–5.5 km/h. Climb (m) ≈ km × incline % × 10, so 4 km at 12% ≈ 480 m. Slow down and stop the belt completely before stepping off |
| **Upright bike** | Now | Get on and off while it's stationary, using only your uninjured hand. Knee slightly bent at the bottom of the stroke. Stay seated, with the injured arm across your stomach or on your thigh |
| **Recumbent bike** (if your gym has one) | Now | The easiest to get on and off. Otherwise, use the upright bike |
| **Outdoor walking** | Now | Tarmac or firm, level gravel, dry and in daylight. On frosty or leafy mornings, use the treadmill ([chapter 12](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md)) |
| **Sled drag (belt)** | Now | The hub assumes your gym has no sled belt, so every circuit uses tempo squats instead. If yours has one, or you buy an optional dip belt (about £15–25) to clip to the sled strap, drags come back at any phase: untick them under Settings → My gym: unavailable exercises |
| **Rower** | Grip cleared at the earliest, with your physio's OK | Two-handed pulling through both wrists, hundreds of times. Steady Zone 2 only, starting at 5–10 minutes. Hard intervals wait for Full clearance. The programme only uses it from Phase 4, as steady Zone 2 |
| **Ski erg** | Grip cleared at the earliest, with your physio's OK | Two-handed pulling from overhead. Same rule: steady work first, and intervals only from Full clearance. The programme only uses it from Phase 4a, as steady Zone 2 |
| **Sled rope pull** | Grip cleared | Pulling a rope loads the forearms. It's in the Phase 3 circuits, once pulling is pain-free and your physio agrees |
| **Sled push** | Full clearance | Force goes back through the hands and wrists, like a wall press. Ask your physio about it specifically |
| **Air bike with moving arms** | Grip cleared at the earliest, steady only | The handles move with the pedals, so you can't ride it with the arm resting |
| **Battle ropes** | Full clearance | Two-handed, repetitive loading through the forearms |
| **Running, jumping, outdoor cycling** | Full clearance | Fall risk |

Between Grip cleared and Phase 4a, the programme doesn't use the rower or ski erg. If your physio agrees, you can swap a few minutes of steady Zone 2 onto them, keeping to the arm pain rule ([chapter 19](19-injury-prevention.md)).

## Circuits as cardio

Cardio doesn't have to mean a machine. A circuit of strength exercises done back to back, at an effort where you can still talk in short sentences, trains the heart **and** the muscles that hiking needs. A 2017 meta-analysis found that resistance circuit training raised VO2max in healthy adults, with the largest effects from about 14–30 sessions over 6–12 weeks **[Moderate evidence]**. Eight weeks of high-intensity circuits improved both aerobic capacity and strength in wildland firefighters, whose job looks a lot like loaded hiking **[Moderate evidence]**.

**The arm-free circuit (open now, optional).** Use it as an occasional swap for a Zone 2 session: 20–30 minutes continuously at Zone 2, walking calmly between stations.

| Station | Dose | Safety note |
|---|---|---|
| `step-up-bw` | 10 per side | On an aerobic step (15–25 cm) or the bottom stair, never a bench. Uninjured hand on an upright or rail. Step down slowly |
| `leg-press` | 15 reps, light | Dead start on the stops, plates loaded as its library entry says. Injured arm across your stomach; stand up with your uninjured hand on the side handle |
| `incline-walk` | 2 min | Start the belt while you're standing on it, uninjured hand lightly on the rail, safety key clipped on |
| `wall-sit` | 30–45 s | Slide up the wall using your legs |
| `calf-raise-single-bw` | 12 per side | Fingertips of your uninjured hand on a support |
| `bike-upright` | 2 min easy | Seated, injured arm resting |

From Phase 2 the programme's own circuits take over, with tempo squats (or belt drags if you have a sled belt or dip belt), carries by the grip ladder, steps and woodchops, and later rope pulls and sandbags, each at its own gate ([chapter 09](09-farmer-strength.md)). Carries follow [the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule). The rule for every circuit stays the same: steady, conversational and repeatable.

## Testing your engine

| Test | How | How often |
|---|---|---|
| **Morning resting HR** | Before getting up, the same method every day, entered in your check-in. The hub tracks your 7-day average, so there's no separate test | Daily |
| **1-mile (1.6 km) walk test** (Progress → Tests) | As fast as you can **without running**, on a flat, dry, measured route or a treadmill at 1%. Record the time, and your finishing HR in the notes | Every 4 weeks |
| **Stair-climber minutes** (optional; not one of the hub's tests, so note it when you log the session) | Same machine and level each time. Climb until your HR stays above the top of your Zone 2 (131 for the example profile) for 30 seconds, your form slips, or you reach 30 minutes | Every 4–8 weeks, if you like |
| **HR drift test** (optional) | See above | Every 4–6 months, from about week 8 |

The 1-mile walk test isn't just a gym habit. In 343 adults aged 30–69, an equation using walk time, finishing HR, age, sex and weight tracked measured VO2max closely (r = 0.92–0.93) **[Strong evidence]**. Your own time and HR, done the same way each time, are enough to track progress. [Chapter 04](04-the-program.md) says when your baseline tests fall.

> [!SAFETY]
> Stop any cardio session and get advice if you have chest pain or tightness, unusual breathlessness, palpitations, dizziness or fainting. Any arm red flag, or arm pain of 7/10 or more, makes the day RED: no training at all, including your physio exercises, until you've been advised. Contact your fracture clinic today, or NHS 111 / 999 as the red-flag guidance says ([chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md)).
