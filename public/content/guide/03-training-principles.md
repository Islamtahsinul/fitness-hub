# Training Principles

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - You get good at exactly what you practise. That's why your plan is built from stairs, single-leg work, long walks, leg strength and (later) carries: the real demands of long hikes and farmer-style carrying.
> - You're an experienced lifter coming back, not starting out. Muscle memory usually brings back what you lost faster than it took to build, so the plan finds your loads in one Re-entry Week and runs full sets from programme week 2.
> - Your default progression is **double progression**: start around a load you could do for 15, add reps within a range, then add a small step of weight.
> - Work hard, but never to failure: most sets stop 1–3 reps short. In Phase 1, quads and glutes get about 10–14 hard sets a week and hamstrings and calves 8–10, with a deload every 5–6 weeks and an easy day whenever your check-in says so.
> - You train around the injury, not through it: legs, engine and arm-free core now; your uninjured arm once your clinic allows; your injured arm only as your physio prescribes. The arm sets the pace.

As an experienced lifter, you know most of these principles. This chapter shows how they're applied to a comeback around a healing arm, so you can make good calls on your own: when to add weight, when to back off, what to do after a bad night's sleep, and why some things wait for a clearance gate. The programme itself is in [The Programme](04-the-program.md); the arm-safe version of each lift is in [Strength Technique](05-strength-technique.md).

## 1. Specificity: you adapt to what you do

Your body adapts specifically to the demands you place on it. Coaches call this the **SAID principle** (Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands) **[Expert opinion]**. Squat and you get better at squatting; walk uphill for an hour and you get better at walking uphill for an hour. General fitness helps, but the closer training looks like the goal, the more directly it carries over.

So start with what your goals actually demand.

| Your goal | What it demands | How your plan trains it |
|---|---|---|
| Long hikes on any terrain | Hours on your feet, sustained climbing, controlled descents, ankles and hips that cope with uneven ground | Zone 2 stair climber and incline walking, a weekly long walk, rear-foot-elevated split squats and step-ups with a slow lowering, Patrick steps, leg extensions and tempo squats for descents, calf and shin work, balance work, later loaded and back-to-back hikes |
| Core stability | Resisting unwanted bending, twisting and side-bending while you move and carry | Dead bugs, hollow holds and breathing now; Pallof presses, woodchops and carries later ([Core & Stability](06-core-and-stability.md)) |
| Farmer strength | Grip, upright posture and strong hips under load | Hip and leg strength now; good-arm grip work once cleared; a staged carry progression after grip clearance ([Farmer Strength](09-farmer-strength.md)) |
| About 5 kg of muscle | Hard sets close to failure, enough weekly volume, a small food surplus, sleep | The dead-start leg press, single-leg press, single-leg hip thrusts and split squats now; full-body hypertrophy from Phase 2 ([Building Muscle](08-building-muscle.md)) |
| "Very functional" | Strength in every direction, getting up and down, carrying awkward things | Single-leg work, side-hip strength and an arm-free circuit now; get-ups, sandbags and rotation later |

### Andy Galpin's nine adaptations

Exercise physiologist Andy Galpin describes nine distinct adaptations you can train, from skill and speed to long-duration endurance, and points out that fat loss is a by-product of training rather than an adaptation ([Huberman Lab, 2022](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/dr-andy-galpin-how-to-build-strength-muscle-size-and-endurance)) **[Expert opinion]**. You can't maximise all nine at once, so your plan picks priorities by phase.

| Adaptation | Priority now (Phases 0–1) | Priority later |
|---|---|---|
| Skill | Low to moderate: you know the lifts; the new skill is setting up every machine one-handed | Each lift that returns after the injury starts light |
| Speed | Low | Low |
| Power | Not yet (no jumping before full clearance) | Phase 4: low box jumps, kettlebell swings |
| Strength | Moderate: the dead-start leg press and machine leg work at 1–3 reps in reserve | High in Phases 3–5 |
| Hypertrophy | High for legs and hips: regaining what the layoff took | High for the whole body from Phase 2 |
| Muscular endurance | Moderate: single-leg work, calf work, the Friday circuit | Carries and circuits |
| Anaerobic power | 30-s bike efforts from Phase 1 (a Zone 3 primer until week 6) | Circuits |
| VO2max | Built indirectly by the 30-s efforts | 4 × 4-minute intervals from Phase 2, uphill from Phase 4 |
| Long-duration endurance | Highest: your hiking base | Long days and back-to-back weekends |

### Peter Attia's four pillars

Peter Attia frames exercise as four pillars: **stability, strength, aerobic performance (Zone 2) and anaerobic output (VO2max or "Zone 5")**, and suggests "backcasting" from what you want to do in your 80s: lift a 30-lb (about 14 kg) suitcase into an overhead bin, get up off the floor using one arm, climb three flights of stairs with 10 lb (about 4.5 kg) of shopping in each hand ([Attia, 2022](https://peterattiamd.com/how-to-train-for-the-centenarian-olympics/)) **[Expert opinion]**. Those tasks are your goals: carrying, stairs, getting up and down.

| Pillar | In your plan now | Later |
|---|---|---|
| Stability | Daily mobility, balance next to a support, arm-free core, hip work | Pallof presses, carries, get-ups, all-terrain balance |
| Strength | Three strength days a week, legs first | Full body, barbells from Phase 4 |
| Aerobic (Zone 2) | Two hiking-engine days and a long walk | 150–240+ minutes a week, long hikes |
| Anaerobic (VO2max) | One short-interval session a week | 4 × 4 and 5 × 4 minutes, uphill intervals |

## 2. Progressive overload

Your body adapts to a training stress plus recovery. Once it has adapted, the same stress maintains fitness but no longer builds it. To keep improving, you gradually increase the demand. That's **progressive overload**, and you've used it for years.

The American College of Sports Medicine's position stand bases load progression on the same idea: once you can do one or two reps more than the target, increase the load by about **2–10%** ([ACSM, 2009](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22progression%20models%20in%20resistance%20training%20for%20healthy%20adults%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. Your plan's steps sit inside that.

There are many ways to add demand. In rough order of how you'll use them:

1. **Reps** at the same load (the first lever in double progression).
2. **Load**, in small steps.
3. **Range and difficulty**: back foot elevated, two legs to one leg, a higher step (within the 15–25 cm limit).
4. **Tempo and pauses**: a slower lowering or a pause in the bottom makes a bodyweight move hard again.
5. **Sets**: added between phases, not week to week.
6. **Cardio**: minutes first, then machine level, incline, pace or pack weight at the same heart-rate zone.

While the bone heals, one rule sits above all of these: **the arm sets the pace**. If anything in the session grumbles at your arm, you don't progress that week, however good your legs feel. The hub's **arm pain rule** for the injured arm is: follow your physio's limit. If you weren't given one, keep pain at 2/10 or below during the exercise, back to your usual level by the next morning. Never train through pain that's increasing.

**One change at a time.** When the plan steps up, it changes one thing in a week, never several. In Phase 1 that means a steeper incline in week 3, machine leg work to 1–2 reps in reserve in week 4, a deload in week 5 and hard bike efforts in week 6. More sets, harder efforts and steeper hills all in the same week is how people coming back from a layoff end up unusually sore, or worse **[Expert opinion]**.

> [!SAFETY]
> Muscle soreness for a day or two after a step up is normal, and most likely in your first two weeks back. Dark (cola-coloured) urine with severe muscle pain or swelling after training is not: get same-day medical advice (possible rhabdomyolysis, a rare breakdown of muscle tissue).

New movements arrive gradually too. After grip clearance you get two weeks of machines and cables before free weights (Phase 2a). After full clearance a 4-week bridge (Phase 4a) brings in hanging, push-ups, swings, sandbags and free-bar squats in the order the exercise library lists their prerequisites. That's not about learning the lifts; it's about letting the forearm meet each new hold gradually.

## 3. Double progression: how you'll progress almost everything

Every strength exercise in the hub has a **rep range** (for example 8–12) and a **target RIR** (for example 2–3). Here's the method:

1. Start around a load you could do for 15, then let double progression find your level within 2 sessions.
2. Each session, try to add a rep or two at the **same** load.
3. When **every set** reaches the **top** of the range at the target RIR with clean form, add the smallest step of weight next session.
4. Drop back to the bottom of the range and build up again.

Here's how it might look on your dead-start leg press, 3 sets of 8–12 (logging the plates only, both sides together). Re-entry Week's first two sets stop 3–4 reps short and the third is the calibration set at 2 in reserve:

| Session | Plates | Reps (set 1, 2, 3) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Re-entry Monday | 60 kg | 12, 12, 17 (calibration set) | Far too light: 10 kg more next time (5 kg a side) |
| Phase 1, week 1 | 70 kg | 12, 12, 12 | Every set at the top: 10 kg more |
| Phase 1, week 2 | 80 kg | 10, 9, 9 | That's your level. Stay at 80 kg |
| Phase 1, week 3 | 80 kg | 12, 11, 10 | Stay |
| Phase 1, week 4 | 80 kg | 12, 12, 12 | 10 kg more next time |
| Phase 1, week 6 | 90 kg | 10, 10, 9 | Stay; build up again |

The numbers are only an example; your logbook sets your own. Two sessions in, the load is right, and from then on it's ordinary double progression. With muscle memory behind you, expect it to move faster in the first months than it did when you first trained **[Expert opinion]**.

Pavel Tsatsouline's 3–5 method uses the same idea: reps or sets within a fixed range before weight, and the last set always 1–2 reps short of technical failure ([Tsatsouline, StrongFirst](https://www.strongfirst.com/the-3-5-method-revisited/)) **[Expert opinion]**.

**Your increments** (the same steps the hub suggests, from the programme rules):

| What | Step |
|---|---|
| Leg press | 10 kg (5 kg a side) |
| Single-leg press | 5 kg |
| Machines and cables | 2.5 kg or one plate |
| Dumbbells | 2 kg per hand |
| Barbell and Smith squat, bench | 2.5 kg |
| Deadlift, RDL, barbell hip thrust | 5 kg |
| Kettlebells | 4 kg |
| Sandbags | 5 kg |
| Sled | 10 kg |

Kettlebells and sandbags jump in big steps, so add reps or sets first. Until Good arm OK is set, staff change your leg-press plates; after that your uninjured hand loads plates of 10 kg or less, and staff do heavier ones.

**When the hub says "Hold".** In deload weeks, on AMBER (easy) days and during a fasting period you've set, the hub shows "Hold: same load, RIR 3–4" and suggests no load or rep increases. During a fasting period, start no new lifts either.

**When there's no weight to add:**
- **Bodyweight moves:** reps, then a slower tempo or a pause, then a harder version (back foot elevated, single leg) or, from Light use / rehab started, your day pack.
- **Fixed-rep strength lifts** (3 × 5, 4 × 4–6): add load when every set stops at least 2 reps short of failure. Start these around a load you could do for 10.
- **Timed holds and carries:** add 5–10 seconds, then add load.
- **Cardio:** follow the minutes in the plan; once they plateau, raise the level, incline or pace at the same zone.

## 4. Effort: reps in reserve (RIR) and RPE

**RIR** is the number of good reps you could still have done at the end of a set. If you stop at 10 and could have managed 12, that's RIR 2. Some coaches use **RPE** (rating of perceived exertion) on a 1–10 scale instead. In the RIR-based version, RPE 9 means 1 rep left, RPE 7 means 3 left and so on, so RPE is simply 10 minus RIR ([Helms et al., 2016; Zourdos et al., 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22repetitions%20in%20reserve%22%20AND%20%28AUTH:%22Zourdos%22%20OR%20AUTH:%22Helms%22%29%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8)).

| RIR | RPE | What it feels like | Where you'll use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 or more | 6 or less | Easy, fast reps | Warm-ups, deloads |
| 3–4 | 6–7 | Moderate; you could clearly do more | Re-entry Week's first two sets, intro weeks, lifts that return after the injury |
| 2–3 | 7–8 | Hard but clean; reps start to slow | Most work sets; everything free-standing; the single-leg press; all upper-body work until Phase 4 |
| 2 | 8 | Hard; the last rep is clearly slower | Your Re-entry Week calibration sets |
| 1–2 | 8–9 | Very hard; the last rep is slow | Machine leg work from Phase 1 week 4 (leg press, leg extension, hamstring curl); hip thrusts and calf raises from Phase 3; upper body only from Phase 4 |
| 0 | 10 | Failure | Not in this programme |

**How good are people at guessing RIR?** Guesses get more accurate the closer you are to failure and on lower-rep sets. In one study, training experience didn't predict accuracy ([Zourdos et al., 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22repetitions%20in%20reserve%22%20AND%20%28AUTH:%22Zourdos%22%20OR%20AUTH:%22Helms%22%29%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8)) **[Moderate evidence]**, so years in the gym don't make you immune, and after a long layoff your sense of RIR will be rusty for a fortnight. That's what the calibration sets are for. Three tips:
- Watch rep speed. When a rep is noticeably slower than the first, you're usually about 2 reps from failure.
- Be extra cautious on high-rep sets (15+), where everyone underestimates what's left. Your bodyweight single-leg work lives here.
- Write your guess down every set. Reviewing the log teaches you your own patterns.

### Why you never need to train to failure

- **Muscle growth.** A 2023 meta-analysis found no clear advantage for training to failure over stopping short, and a 2024 trial found similar growth at 1–2 RIR as at failure ([Refalo et al., 2023 and 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=Refalo%20proximity%20to%20failure%20hypertrophy%20meta-analysis&format=json&resultType=core)). Growth may creep up closer to failure, while strength gains look similar across a wide range of RIR ([Robinson et al., 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=Refalo%20proximity%20to%20failure%20hypertrophy%20meta-analysis&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. Hence RIR 1–3: close enough to grow, far enough to stay safe.
- **Fatigue.** Neuromuscular fatigue climbs steeply near failure **[Moderate evidence]** (Refalo et al., 2023), and Pavel's school says it bluntly: "never train to failure", work at about RPE 7–9 and finish fresh ([Neupert, StrongFirst](https://www.strongfirst.com/10-reasons-to-avoid-training-to-failure/)) **[Expert opinion]**.
- **For you specifically.** Failure is where form breaks, balance wobbles and people hold their breath and strain: exactly what you're avoiding before full clearance. That's why only stable machine leg work goes to 1–2 in reserve, and only from Phase 1 week 4.

## 5. Tempo notation

The hub writes tempo the usual way: **3-1-1-0** is a 3-s lowering, a 1-s pause at the bottom, a 1-s lift and no pause at the top (X = as fast as you can with control). Calf raises at **2-1-1-1** pause in the stretch, so you can't bounce out of the bottom on the Achilles' spring.

A meta-analysis found similar growth with rep durations from 0.5 to 8 seconds; only very slow reps (over about 10 s) looked worse ([Schoenfeld et al., 2015](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22meta-analysis%20to%20determine%20the%20dose%20response%20for%20strength%20development%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22effect%20of%20repetition%20duration%20during%20resistance%20training%20on%20muscle%20hypertrophy%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. So tempo here isn't magic. It does three practical jobs for you:
- **Control.** Slow lowering keeps every rep in the groove, which matters when you can't grab anything if you wobble.
- **Joint-friendly overload.** A 3-s lowering and a pause make bodyweight single-leg work hard enough to count, while the arm limits what you can hold.
- **Descent strength.** Long downhills are thousands of controlled lowering reps for your quads.

## 6. Volume and frequency

**Volume** here means **hard sets per muscle per week**. A hard set is a work set that finishes within about 1–4 reps of failure **[Expert opinion]**. Warm-up and ramp-up sets don't count.

- A meta-analysis found a graded dose-response: more weekly sets, more growth, with each extra weekly set adding a little (about 0.4% more gain) ([Schoenfeld et al., 2017](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22dose-response%20relationship%20between%20weekly%20resistance%20training%20volume%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- Galpin suggests about **10 hard sets per muscle per week** as a minimum for growth, with 15–20 often more productive ([Huberman Lab, 2022](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/dr-andy-galpin-how-to-build-strength-muscle-size-and-endurance)) **[Expert opinion]**.
- Cramming too many sets into one workout seems to backfire ([Benito et al., 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22effect%20of%20resistance%20training%20on%20whole-body%20muscle%20growth%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. So volume is spread over the week.

**Frequency** matters less than total volume: with weekly volume equal, training a muscle once or three times a week grows it about the same ([Schoenfeld et al., 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22how%20many%20times%20per%20week%20should%20a%20muscle%20be%20trained%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22strength%20and%20hypertrophy%20adaptations%20between%20low-%20vs.%20high-load%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. Two or three sessions simply keep each one shorter and better. The NHS minimum is strength work on 2 days a week ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/physical-activity-guidelines-for-adults-aged-19-to-64/)); you'll do 3.

**Weekly hard sets in your plan** (a full week of each phase, counted from the programme file). A set counts fully for its main movers, such as quads and glutes in a split squat, and half for helpers. Not counted: circuits, timed holds, carries, swings, jumps, get-ups, core work, and the knee and shin armour (Patrick steps, tibialis raises), which are there for durability rather than size.

| Phase | Quads | Glutes | Hamstrings | Calves | Upper body |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 Re-entry Week | 3 sets of each main lift: 2 at RIR 3–4, then 1 calibration set at RIR 2 | | | | none (arm-free) |
| 1 Foundation | about 12 | about 13 | about 9 | about 8 | none; 15 sets for your uninjured arm in each good-arm add-on (3 a week) |
| 2a Machines First | about 12 | about 16 | about 8 | about 2 | chest 3, back 10, shoulders 4, arms 6 |
| 2 Rebuild | about 12 | about 15 | about 8 | about 3 | chest 6, back 10, shoulders 7, arms 9 |
| 3 Build | about 13 | about 16 | about 8 | about 3 | chest 6, back 9, shoulders 6, arms 13 |
| 4a Bridge | about 12 | about 14 | about 7 | about 2 | chest 6, back 9, shoulders 3, arms 8 |
| 4 Forge | about 11 | about 10 | about 4 | about 2 | chest 6, back 6, shoulders 6, arms 8 |
| 5 Mission | about 10 | about 10 | about 4 | about 2 | chest 3, back 6, shoulders 5, arms 6 |

In Phase 1 every leg muscle is trained twice a week, inside the ranges that suit a returning lifter: quads and glutes about 10–14 hard sets, hamstrings and calves about 8–10. That's deliberately moderate: muscle you've built before tends to come back on less work than it took to build, and the first fortnight after a long layoff is when too much volume causes trouble (section 11) **[Expert opinion]**. From Phase 2, stair climbing, pack walks and hikes carry most of the calf work, and in Phases 4–5 deadlifts, swings and hills much of the hinge work; the count leaves those out.

**What about all the cardio?** Doing endurance and strength training together ("concurrent training") does not appear to blunt muscle growth or maximal strength. It does dampen explosive power a little, especially when both are done in the same session ([Schumann et al., 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22compatibility%20of%20concurrent%20aerobic%20and%20strength%20training%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Strong evidence]**. Your plan keeps most cardio easy (Zone 2) and puts hard intervals on non-lifting days.

## 7. Deloads: planned easy weeks

A **deload** is a planned week of reduced training stress, designed to clear fatigue so you can keep progressing ([Bell et al., 2023](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22deload%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22deloading%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8)) **[Expert opinion]**. In a survey of 246 strength and physique athletes, deloads lasted about a week and came roughly every 5–6 weeks. Athletes cut sets and reps and reduced load and effort, but kept training frequency ([Rogerson et al., 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22deload%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22deloading%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8)) **[Expert opinion]**. Your plan uses the same rhythm.

What does the research say about results? In trained lifters, a one-week break midway through a 9-week programme made no appreciable difference to muscle size. It may have cost a little lower-body strength ([Coleman et al., 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%28TITLE:%22deload%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22deloading%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=8)) **[Moderate evidence]**. So a deload is cheap insurance. You lose almost nothing, and you gain recovery, joint and tendon tolerance, and motivation. That matters all the more because you're also healing a fracture.

**How you deload** (the hub applies the first three automatically on deload weeks, and holds your loads):
- **Half the sets** (the hub halves them, rounding up), with the same exercises and loads, stopping 3–4 reps short.
- **Easy everything:** the hub halves the interval rounds, and this plan keeps every zone at Zone 2 that week, so circuits shrink to 5–6 easy minutes.
- **Shorter cardio:** about 60–70% of the usual minutes. These numbers are already written into each week of the plan.
- Mobility and daily walking as normal.

**Your planned deloads, every 5–6 weeks:** Phase 1 week 5, Phase 2 week 3, Phase 3 weeks 3 and 9, Phase 4 weeks 5, 10 and 15, and Phase 5 week 5. Phase 5 also treats its taper (week 9) and Mission week (week 10) as deloads in the gym. While a phase repeats because you're waiting for a clearance, the repeat keeps one deload every 5–6 weeks: Phase 1 repeats weeks 4–8 and Phase 3 weeks 7–12. When a new phase starts with a 2-set intro week, that week works as a lighter week. Take a deload early if your readiness is AMBER on 3 or more days in a week, or your main lifts drop two sessions running: the hub suggests it, and the call is yours.

## 8. Autoregulation: readiness and easy days

A plan is a forecast; your body on the day is the weather. **Autoregulation** means adjusting to the weather. RIR does part of this automatically: on a good day RIR 2 might be 92 kg, on a rough day 85 kg. The morning **Check-in** does the rest. The rule below is the same everywhere in the hub and guide, and a check-in with only your weight in it doesn't count.

| Status | Trigger | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **RED** | Any arm red flag ticked (see [Your Arm](18-arm-recovery.md)), **or** arm pain 7/10 or more | Stop. No training today, including your physio exercises, until you've been advised. Contact your fracture clinic today, or NHS 111 / 999 as the red-flag guidance says. |
| **AMBER** | Any one of: morning resting heart rate at or above your 7-day average + 5 bpm (counted once there are 5 readings since you last changed how you measure it); sleep under 6 h; arm pain 4/10 or more; arm pain 2 or more points above its 7-day average (with at least 3 earlier readings); soreness 7/10 or more; energy 2/5 or less; a day inside a fasting period you've set | Easy day: halve the sets, Zone 2 only, no intervals, hold loads. |
| **GREEN** | None of the above | Train as planned. |

Rising arm pain can be the red flag "pain getting worse": if it keeps rising, or you're unsure, call your fracture clinic today. On GREEN days the hub's coach notes only point out trends; they never send you to an easy day. Fasting days are AMBER automatically: train after you've broken your fast if you can, and stop at any dizziness or light-headedness ([Nutrition](14-nutrition.md) covers eating around the fast).

A few more autoregulation habits:
- **Feeling amazing?** Do the plan anyway, and bank the extra energy for next week's progress **[Expert opinion]**. This is the hardest habit for an experienced lifter on the way back: the legs feel ready long before the arm is.
- **Ill?** Huberman's protocol suggests cutting a session by at least half for mild symptoms, skipping it if you're truly sick, and ramping back up over 3–7 days ([Huberman Lab](https://www.hubermanlab.com/newsletter/foundational-fitness-protocol)) **[Expert opinion]**; the missed-session rule in [The Programme](04-the-program.md) builds on that.
- **Pain** is information, not a challenge. The pain rules are in [Injury Prevention & Pain Rules](19-injury-prevention.md).

## 9. Warm-ups that do a job

Warming up improved performance in 79% of the outcomes measured in a meta-analysis ([Fradkin et al., 2010](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22effects%20of%20warming-up%20on%20physical%20performance%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. Yours is short and purposeful.

**Strength days (about 17 minutes):**
1. **5 minutes easy on the upright bike**, injured arm resting on your thigh.
2. **The daily mobility routine (10 minutes):** a standing block first (ankles, hip circles, single-leg balance, a supported deep squat, seated upper-back turns), then one trip to the floor for 90/90 hip switches and breathing, finishing with the half-kneeling hip flexor stretch (the couch stretch can take its place once Light use / rehab started is set). It's set out in [Mobility & Movement Quality](07-mobility.md).
3. **Ramp-up sets** of the session's first lift: about 50% of your working load × 8, then about 75% × 4. They don't count as work sets. When the leg press comes first (Strength A), the ramp-up is 10 easy box squats, so the plates don't change.

**Cardio days:** a 4-minute leg-swing and hip-circle warm-up, then the first 3–5 minutes on the machine at an easy pace. **Walks:** the first 5–10 minutes easy. Mobility follows engine sessions and walks as a cool-down.

## 10. Strength is a skill (Pavel)

Pavel Tsatsouline's StrongFirst school teaches that **strength is a skill**. You build it through patient, precise practice, and quality and control matter more than punishment ([Woods, StrongFirst](https://www.strongfirst.com/strength-is-a-skill/)) **[Expert opinion]**. You already have the skill; the practice keeps it sharp while the arm heals:

- **Crisp reps.** Every rep of a set should look the same. When one doesn't, the set is over.
- **Tension and breathing.** A moderate brace and a sharp exhale on the effort, never a maximal breath-hold before clearance.
- **Practice, not workouts.** Many good reps, spread out, never grinding.
- **Grease the groove (GTG).** StrongFirst's version: one good rep per mini-set, at least 10–15 minutes apart, at about half your maximum effort, on at least 3 days a week, and no more than two skills at a time ([Smith, StrongFirst](https://www.strongfirst.com/one-good-rep-perform-perfect-pistol/)) **[Expert opinion]**.

The same article makes a point that matters for you: **connective tissue adapts more slowly than muscle, so loading should rise gradually** ([Moseley, StrongFirst](https://www.strongfirst.com/the-three-pillars-for-healthy-aging-meet-the-strongfirst-standards/)) **[Expert opinion]**. Tendons, ligaments and healing bone set the speed limit, not your enthusiasm or your old numbers.

**Your GTG options by gate:**

| Gate | GTG option | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| Before full clearance | None outside the gym. Your ramp-up box squats are your squat-pattern practice. | Extra unsupervised practice adds fall risk while the bone heals |
| Full clearance, grip at least 90% | Short dead hangs or single pull-ups; a few kettlebell swings; box pistols to a high box next to a support | Far from failure; at least 45 minutes apart; only after the Phase 4a bridge |

## 11. Coming back after a layoff: muscle memory

This plan is written for an experienced lifter returning after an injury layoff: years of consistent training, then weeks or months with no real loading while the bone heals.

**What a layoff costs.** In a meta-analysis of training cessation, losses of strength and power grew with the length of the break, and were larger in inactive people than in recreational athletes ([Bosquet et al., 2013](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22effect%20of%20training%20cessation%20on%20muscular%20performance%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. If your legs have had walks, not loading, since the injury, expect them to feel weaker than you remember.

**What comes back fast.** Strength is partly a skill, and the skill outlasts the muscle. In one trial, ten weeks of single-leg training raised strength and muscle size; after 20 weeks off, size was back to baseline but strength was still clearly above where it started, which the authors put down to long-lasting motor learning ([Psilander et al., 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22training%2C%20detraining%2C%20and%20retraining%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22myonuclear%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. In an older study, women who stopped training for 30–32 weeks kept part of their strength and fibre size, and 6 weeks of retraining grew their fast-twitch fibres significantly ([Staron et al., 1991](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22detraining%20and%20retraining%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22strength%22%20AND%20TITLE:%22women%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**. And young men who took 3-week breaks between 6-week bench-press blocks ended 24 weeks with the same muscle and strength gains as men who never stopped ([Ogasawara et al., 2013](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22comparison%20of%20muscle%20hypertrophy%20following%206-month%20of%20continuous%20and%20periodic%20strength%20training%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**.

**Why muscle might remember.** Two explanations are debated:
- **Extra nuclei in the muscle fibres.** In mice, the nuclei that training adds were kept through disuse and seemed to make retraining easier ([Bruusgaard et al., 2010](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22myonuclei%20acquired%20by%20overload%20exercise%22&format=json&resultType=core)). But a 2022 meta-analysis of human and animal studies found these nuclei are not permanent in people, which weakens this explanation for humans ([Rahmati et al., 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22muscle%20memory%22%20AND%20%28TITLE:%22review%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22retraining%22%20OR%20TITLE:%22detraining%22%29&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=10)) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **An epigenetic memory.** In a study of people who built muscle, lost it, then trained again, the muscle carried chemical marks on its DNA from the first round of growth, and the second round of loading produced the biggest gains ([Seaborne et al., 2018](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22epigenetic%20memory%20of%20hypertrophy%22&format=json&resultType=core)) **[Moderate evidence]**.

Not every study finds faster regrowth: in Psilander's trial the previously trained leg regained size no faster than the untrained one. The honest summary: skill and much of the strength come back within weeks; muscle you've had before usually comes back faster than it took to build, but the speed varies **[Expert opinion]**.

**What that means for your plan:**
1. **Find your loads quickly.** Re-entry Week starts around a load you could do for 15, then double progression finds your level within 2 sessions.
2. **Moderate volume is enough to regain.** About 10–14 hard sets for quads and glutes and 8–10 for hamstrings and calves rebuild what you had; more volume waits until you're past it.
3. **Respect the first fortnight.** Volume your muscles haven't seen for months, with slow lowering on almost everything, is the classic recipe for severe soreness. That's why Re-entry Week stops short and why the rhabdomyolysis warning above matters most in weeks 1–2.
4. **Your old numbers are not this year's targets.** Connective tissue and the healing bone lag behind muscle, and your injured arm sets the pace for anything it touches.
5. **Eat to regain.** Below your usual pre-injury weight, the hub's weight band is a little faster (+0.25 to +0.5 kg a week), then it's the slower lean-gain band ([Building 5 kg of Muscle](08-building-muscle.md), [Nutrition](14-nutrition.md)). Sleep 7–9 hours too ([Sleep, Recovery & Readiness](17-sleep-and-recovery.md)).

## 12. Training around an injury

This plan is built around a healing arm fracture (for example a forearm fracture, possibly with surgical plates). The fracture clinic and your physio are the authority on the arm; this programme fits around whatever they say, and anything they prescribe overrides it. The principles:

1. **Protect the arm completely until each gate is cleared.** Nothing grips, holds, pushes, pulls, braces or bears weight through it. Nothing needs two hands. You get into and out of machines, and up from the floor, without it.
2. **Fall risk is the biggest threat.** No jumping, sprints, wobble boards, rough or slippery ground, or outdoor cycling before full clearance. Balance work always happens beside a support your uninjured hand can touch.
3. **Moderate bracing only.** Breathe out on the effort; no maximal breath-hold straining before clearance.
4. **Train everything you safely can.** Legs, hips, core and your aerobic engine are all trainable right now, and they are most of what hiking needs.
5. **Use your uninjured arm once allowed.** Training one limb can help the opposite, unused limb hold on to strength (**cross-education**). The trials are small and may not match your injury, so treat it as a helpful bonus, not a treatment; the evidence is summarised in [Your Arm](18-arm-recovery.md) **[Moderate evidence]**. Your plan's good-arm block (Monday, Wednesday and Friday, about 20 minutes, seated, 3 sets at a moderate 2–3 reps in reserve) switches on only once Good arm OK is set and runs through Phases 0–1, including any repeated weeks. It finishes with wrist and grip work, because the carry-over seems greatest for the same movement **[Expert opinion]**.

> [!SAFETY]
> If you notice any red flag — increasing arm pain, new swelling, redness or heat, discharge from the wound, fever, numbness or tingling, a colour change or coldness in the hand, a feeling that something has given way, or any fall onto the arm — stop and contact the fracture clinic or NHS 111. Full details are in [Your Arm](18-arm-recovery.md).
