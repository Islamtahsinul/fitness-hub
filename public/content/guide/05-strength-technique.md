# Strength Technique — Movement Patterns

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - You know how to squat, hinge, lunge, push, pull and carry. This chapter is about what changes while your injured forearm heals: the arm-safe version of each pattern **now**, and what each gate unlocks.
> - **Now**, before any clearance, you can train every pattern that doesn't need your injured arm: the dead-start leg press and single-leg press, rear-foot-elevated split squats, step-ups, single-leg hip thrusts and RDLs, side-lying hip work, dead bugs and walking.
> - Each gate unlocks the next version of each pattern. The clinic and your physio move you up a level; pain or swelling moves you back one.
> - Until full clearance: a moderate brace with steady breathing, a slow lowering, 2–3 reps in reserve on anything free-standing, your uninjured hand within reach of a support for anything on one leg, and nothing you could fall from.
> - Every lift that returns after the injury starts light, for the forearm's sake rather than for skill. A physio's or coach's eye on your first sessions with each new hold is worth booking.

## How to use this chapter

Each pattern below gives the one thing that changes for you, the arm-safe version **now**, and what each gate unlocks. The exercise pages in the hub have the set-up, cues and dose; [The Programme](04-the-program.md) decides what you do and when.

| Gate | What it unlocks for technique work |
|---|---|
| Now | Lower body, core and cardio with your injured arm completely out of the job |
| Good arm OK | Seated one-arm machine and cable work with your uninjured arm |
| Light use / rehab started | Physio rehab for your injured arm; light rucksack walking; the couch stretch |
| Grip cleared | Light-to-moderate holding with both hands: dumbbells, machines, cables, the Smith machine, and barbell RDLs and hip thrusts. Still no weight on the wrist, no hanging and no impact |
| Full clearance | Free-bar squats, deadlifts and bench (Phase 4a onwards), push-ups, planks, hanging, kettlebell swings and get-ups, sandbags, jumps and heavy loads |

The order within each pattern (machine or bodyweight, then dumbbells, then barbells, then the hardest versions) is the order in which your forearm takes load. Skipping a step saves weeks but risks costing you months **[Expert opinion]**.

> [!SAFETY]
> The arm rule for any exercise that uses your injured arm: pain stays within your physio's limit during the set (if you haven't been given one, 2/10 or less), it's back to normal by the next morning, and there's no new swelling. If not, drop back a step. Arm pain of 4/10 or more is an AMBER day in the hub. Any red flag (worsening pain, new swelling, redness, discharge, fever, numbness, colour change, a fall on the arm) means stop and contact the fracture clinic or NHS 111. The NHS also advises not lifting heavy items until you've been told it's safe. See [Your Arm](18-arm-recovery.md) and [Injury Prevention & Pain Rules](19-injury-prevention.md).

## Bracing and breathing

You know how to brace. What changes until full clearance is how hard. How to rebuild the brace from 90/90 breathing is in [Core & Stability](06-core-and-stability.md).

### Now (until full clearance): moderate brace, keep breathing

- Use about a third of your maximum tension: enough that your trunk doesn't move, but not so much that your face goes red.
- **Exhale on the effort.** Breathe out through pursed lips, or with a "tsss" hiss, through the hardest part of each rep. Breathe in on the way down or at the top.
- Never hold your breath for a whole set or strain against a closed throat.

Straining with a held breath (the Valsalva manoeuvre) sends blood pressure soaring. In a classic study that measured arterial pressure directly, experienced bodybuilders doing heavy leg presses to failure averaged peaks of 320/250 mmHg **[Moderate evidence]** (a small study) (MacDougall et al., 1985). A review found the highest internal pressures in squats and deadlifts **[Moderate evidence]** (Blazek et al., 2019). You don't need those peaks while your bone heals. The leg press is where experienced lifters hold their breath without noticing, so breathe out as you press on every rep, starting from the stops.

### Later (after full clearance): full brace for heavy sets

For heavy squats and deadlifts in sets of 1–5, take a big breath at the top and brace hard, around 8/10. Hold it through the hardest part of **one** rep, breathe out near the top, then take a fresh breath for the next rep. Lighter work keeps the moderate brace. If you have, or are told you have, high blood pressure or a heart or eye condition, ask your GP before doing any breath-hold lifting.

> [!WARNING]
> Seeing stars, a pounding headache or a red face after a set means you strained too hard. Stop for the day, and next time breathe out earlier in the rep.

## Tempo, effort and rest: the technique view

Tempo notation, reps in reserve (RIR), volume and rest are explained in [Training Principles](03-training-principles.md). For technique while the arm heals, four points matter:

- **Lower slowly (about 3 s).** It kills the bounce and makes a lurch (and a reflex grab with your injured hand) less likely. It also trains the braking strength your knees need downhill, and it's what makes bodyweight single-leg work hard enough to count.
- **Lift smoothly and with intent,** as fast as you can while staying in control. Swings, slams and jumps are fast on purpose, and they're full-clearance work.
- **Stop when the form changes, not when you can't move.** Every lift that returns after the injury gets a light first week at RIR 3 or more. Until full clearance, free-standing and balance-heavy work, the single-leg press and all upper-body work stay at RIR 2–3. Machine leg work (leg press, leg extension, hamstring curl) goes to RIR 1–2 from Phase 1 week 4, and hip thrusts and calf raises from Phase 3.
- **Rest long enough to repeat the quality:** 2–3 minutes between sets of big lifts, 45–90 s on small ones.

## The eight patterns, arm-safe

### 1. Squat

**What changes for you:** no bar on your back and nothing in your hands until the gates say so. The dead-start leg press is your heavy squat; bodyweight work gets its difficulty from tempo, pauses and range. Deep squats themselves are fine for healthy knees: a biomechanics review found that, progressed sensibly, they don't add injury risk to cartilage and ligaments **[Moderate evidence]** (Hartmann et al., 2013).

**Now:** the dead-start leg press (injured arm across your stomach, never hands on your thighs; the sled rests on the stops between sets, and your uninjured hand takes the side handle when you stand up), box squats with your injured arm across your stomach as the Monday ramp-up, tempo squats in the circuit, wall sits, leg extensions, a Spanish squat if your gym has a band, and the supported deep-squat hold.

**After each gate**
- **Good arm OK and Light use / rehab started:** no change to the patterns. Your uninjured hand can now load leg-press plates of 10 kg or less (one at a time; staff do heavier ones), and from Light use your day pack adds hands-free load to calf raises, split squats and step-ups.
- **Grip cleared:** goblet squat (both palms cupping a dumbbell, the gentlest hold), then the Smith squat (empty bar first, stops set) with a moderate brace. Free-weight squats produced more leg-muscle activity than Smith squats in a small study **[Moderate evidence]** (Schwanbeck et al., 2009), so the Smith is a bridge back to the free bar, not the destination.
- **Full clearance:** the free-bar back squat from Phase 4a (empty 20 kg bar first, safety arms set), then heavier back squats with a brief full brace on low-rep sets, plus pogo hops and low box jumps for landing skill from Phase 4.

### 2. Hinge

**What changes for you:** no bar or bell in your hands until grip clearance, so the hinge is trained one leg at a time and through bridges, thrusts and curls.

**Now:** the single-leg RDL next to a rack you can touch with the fingertips of your uninjured hand, the single-leg hip thrust and the adductor-squeeze bridge, the hamstring curl, and the 45° back extension with your arms folded, if your gym has one.

**After each gate**
- **Grip cleared:** kettlebell deadlift (a dumbbell in each hand if your gym has no kettlebells), then the dumbbell RDL, then the barbell RDL from the rack, plus the barbell hip thrust. No straps and no mixed grip: your grip is the warning light for the healing forearm.
- **Full clearance:** StrongFirst's order is deadlift, then two-hand swing, then one-hand swing **[Expert opinion]** (Macek, StrongFirst), so you go kettlebell deadlift → two-hand swing → one-hand swing, plus the barbell deadlift from Phase 4 (light first, then heavier with a brief full brace), the assisted Nordic curl and sandbag to shoulder. In one lab study, swings with a 16 kg bell produced an estimated 3,200 N of low-back compression, so your hinge must be sound and your forearm ready first **[Moderate evidence]** (a small study) (McGill & Marshall, 2012). The bottom of a swing is a deep hike pass, not a squat, and the top is a standing plank **[Expert opinion]** (Jones; Yoon, StrongFirst). Programmes that include the Nordic curl roughly halve hamstring injuries in athletes **[Strong evidence]** (van Dyk et al., 2019).

### 3. Single-leg

**What changes for you:** until full clearance, every single-leg exercise happens next to a rack or wall your uninjured hand can touch (a safety rule, not a suggestion), and every step is an aerobic step (15–25 cm) or the bottom stair, never a bench. Bodyweight single-leg work is worth your time: in one study, rear-foot-elevated split squats matched back squats for strength gains **[Moderate evidence]** (a small study) (Speirs et al., 2016).

**Now:** the rear-foot-elevated split squat (uninjured hand on the upright before you lift the back foot onto the bench), the split squat, step-ups, lateral step-ups, step-downs and Patrick steps, all on the aerobic step or bottom stair, plus the single-leg press. The box pistol waits for full clearance because it has the highest fall risk.

**After each gate**
- **Grip cleared:** dumbbell split squats, Bulgarian split squats and step-ups. Until full clearance, hold **one** dumbbell in your injured hand with your uninjured hand on the rack (or wear your day pack for step-ups), and keep step-ups on the aerobic step. Your legs still get equal work, and your trunk gets an anti-lean challenge too.
- **Full clearance:** a dumbbell in each hand, higher boxes or a bench up to knee height, plus walking lunges (moving on one leg with both hands full) and loaded step-ups with a rucksack.

### 4. Push

**What changes for you:** stacked wrists matter more than ever. The handle or bar sits low in your palm over the forearm bones, with no bending back at the wrist.

**Now:** nothing pushes through your injured arm. Your legs (the leg press) and trunk (dead bugs) do the pushing for now.

**After each gate**
- **Good arm OK:** the one-arm chest press in your good-arm add-on (the seated one-arm shoulder press and triceps pushdown are library alternatives). Uninjured arm only, seated, injured arm across your stomach. Why it's worth doing is in [Your Arm](18-arm-recovery.md).
- **Light use / rehab started:** rehab for your injured arm as prescribed by your physio. No pressing yet.
- **Grip cleared:** the physio-graded wall press, then the chest press machine, then the dumbbell bench press, plus the dumbbell shoulder press and triceps pushdown. There's no rush back to the barbell: machines and free weights build muscle about equally **[Moderate evidence]** (Haugen et al., 2023).
- **Full clearance:** incline push-ups on a high bar, lowered a notch at a time, then push-ups, and the barbell bench press from Phase 4 (empty bar first, safety arms set). Several crisp sets of 3–8, never to failure **[Expert opinion]** (Macek, StrongFirst).

### 5. Pull

**What changes for you:** neutral (palms-facing) handles wherever you can, straight wrists, and a stack that's resting before you let go.

**Now:** no arm pulling. Legs-only sled drags need a sled belt or an optional dip belt (see [The Programme](04-the-program.md)); without one, tempo squats take their place.

**After each gate**
- **Good arm OK:** the one-arm cable row and seated curl in your add-on (the one-arm lat pulldown is a library alternative).
- **Grip cleared:** row machine → seated cable row → lat pulldown → chest-supported dumbbell row, plus face pulls and dumbbell curls (hammer grip first).
- **Full clearance:** the Smith inverted row (bar high) and feet-supported hangs first, because your hands take part of your bodyweight. Then dead hangs → band-assisted pull-ups → pull-ups, plus hanging knee raises.

### 6. Carry

**What changes for you:** two things. No straps, because they shift the load onto the wrist; and the injured hand sets the weight for both hands until the 90% rung. In a lab comparison of strongman events, carrying challenged the trunk differently from lifting, so the authors suggested adding carries to lifting programmes **[Moderate evidence]** (a small study) (McGill et al., 2009). Splitting 30 kg between two hands cut low-back compression by 44% compared with carrying it all in one **[Moderate evidence]** (a small study) (McGill et al., 2013), so the one-hand suitcase carry is the hard core exercise and the farmer's carry lets you carry more for less spinal load.

**Now:** nothing in your hands, and everyday carrying follows [the one carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule). Build the hips and trunk that carries depend on: single-leg balance, side-lying hip abduction, the adductor-squeeze bridge and dead bugs.

**After each gate**
- **Good arm OK:** still nothing walking in your hands. Training carries wait for grip clearance, so the library's good-hand suitcase carry is gated there too, and even then it's only for when your physio suggests it: with your uninjured hand holding the weight, nothing is free to break a stumble.
- **Light use / rehab started:** light-pack walks on good paths. The pack takes the load, not your hands.
- **Grip cleared:** suitcase carries (your injured hand sets the weight and your uninjured hand uses the same), then light-to-moderate farmer's carries, plus plate pinches, all by the grip ladder; each rung counts only when your physio has measured it or OK'd the step.
- **Full clearance:** heavy farmer's carries, sandbag bear-hug and shoulder carries, and loaded-pack hikes. Heavy carries also wait for the 90% rung. The targets come from the standards table in [Farmer Strength](09-farmer-strength.md).

### 7. Rotate and anti-rotate

**What changes for you:** nothing in your hands means rotation work is anti-rotation through the hips and trunk for now: hips and shoulders square while you move on one leg, and the turn coming from hips and upper back when it returns.

**Now:** dead bugs, McGill curl-ups, tuck hollow holds, reverse crunches, 90/90 breathing, seated thoracic rotation, side-lying hip work and single-leg balance. See [Core & Stability](06-core-and-stability.md).

**After each gate**
- **Good arm OK:** the one-arm cable row becomes an anti-rotation drill too, if you keep your shoulders square.
- **Grip cleared:** Pallof presses, slow cable woodchops and suitcase carries.
- **Full clearance:** bird dogs, planks, side planks, Copenhagen planks, get-ups, one-arm swings and slams. Do the get-up with an empty fist, then with a shoe balanced on your fist, before you use any weight: StrongFirst teaches it shoe first and keeps the weight very light at the start **[Expert opinion]** (Macek; Ross, StrongFirst), and for you it's also the gentlest way to load the wrist overhead. The long-term Simple & Sinister standard is 100 one-hand swings in 5 minutes and 10 get-ups in 10 minutes, at 32 kg for men, or 24 kg swings and 16 kg get-ups for women **[Expert opinion]** (Myers, StrongFirst). A programme built on the Copenhagen adductor exercise lowered the risk of groin problems by 41% in a large trial in footballers **[Strong evidence]** (Harøy et al., 2019).

### 8. Gait: walking, climbing and descending

**What changes for you:** your injured arm can't catch you, so the machines and the ground are chosen to make a fall unlikely. On the treadmill and stair climber until full clearance, your uninjured hand rests lightly on the rail (a touch, not a lean), your injured arm stays across your stomach and the safety key is clipped on. Start and stop with the belt or stairs still, and never step onto a moving belt. Use incline rather than speed; on descents, shorter steps and soft knees.

**Now:** walks on flat, well-surfaced paths, incline walking, the stair climber (uninjured hand lightly on the rail) and step-ups. Stay off rough, wet or slippery ground until full clearance.

**After each gate**
- **Light use / rehab started:** light-pack walks on good paths: a light day pack, 2–4 kg to start, put on seated, uninjured arm first.
- **Grip cleared:** trekking poles put weight through your wrists, so they wait for the 70% grip rung and your physio's OK, with no wrist strap on the injured side. See [Hiking Skills, Kit & Mountain Safety](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md).
- **Full clearance:** let go of the rails once you feel steady; loaded-pack hikes and rough terrain, plus pogo hops, low box jumps and broad jumps for springy ankles and landing skill. See [Training for Long-Distance Hiking](11-hiking-training.md).

## Setting up machines safely one-handed

Until Grip cleared, every set-up job is done by your uninjured hand, your legs or the gym staff. The changing-room and travel habits that go with this are in [Injury Prevention & Pain Rules](19-injury-prevention.md).

- **Leg press (dead start):** ask staff once to set the bottom stops at your bottom position (knees about 90°, lower back flat) and to leave the catches released. The sled then rests on the stops: sit in, press from them, finish every set back on them, and step out. You never touch a catch. Staff load the plates until Good arm OK is set; after that your uninjured hand loads plates of 10 kg or less, one at a time, close to your body, and staff do heavier ones. Injured arm across your stomach, never hands on your thighs; stand up with your uninjured hand on the side handle.
- **Pins and seats:** stand where your uninjured hand is nearest the pin, and adjust everything before you sit down. Ask staff for any lever that needs two hands or a hard pull.
- **Cables:** many gyms have no ankle straps, so your side-hip and inner-thigh work is side-lying hip abduction (lying on your uninjured side for the leg on your injured side; standing at the rack for the other leg) and the adductor-squeeze bridge.
- **Your injured arm:** across your stomach, away from moving parts and pinch points. Keep wearing any splint you've been told to use.
- **Getting down to and up from the floor:** the method is in [Mobility & Movement Quality](07-mobility.md#getting-to-and-from-the-floor). Batch your floor work so each strength session needs one trip down, and before full clearance your injured hand never goes on the floor.
- **Smith machines and barbells** wait for Grip cleared, because hooking, unhooking and steadying a bar takes two hands; free-bar squats, deadlifts and bench wait for full clearance.

## Filming yourself

You know your lifts; the camera checks the things the injury changes. Prop your phone at hip height, 2–3 m away, side-on for split squats, hinges and presses, and front-on for knee path.

- **What to check:** that your uninjured hand only touches the support, your injured arm stays across your stomach, your knee tracks over your middle toes on single-leg work, and the tempo matches the plan.
- **When:** your first session of each new exercise, then every few weeks on your main lifts. Film a late set, because fatigue shows faults.
- **Fix one thing at a time.** Cues about the effect of a movement ("push the floor away") tend to help more than cues about body parts **[Moderate evidence]** (McNevin et al., 2000).
- **Etiquette:** don't film other people, and check your gym's filming policy.

## When to get a coached session

1. **Your first grip-cleared sessions.** Ask your physio, or a coach who knows your gates, to watch your first wall presses, goblet squats and carries. This is about the arm, not your technique.
2. **Your first barbell squat, deadlift and bench back after full clearance.** One session is enough to check set-up, safety arms and how the forearm copes with the bar.
3. **Kettlebell swings and get-ups, if they're new to you.** Ideally see a StrongFirst-certified (SFG) instructor.
4. **When a fault survives two sessions** of filming and correcting.
5. **When pain, not effort, keeps showing up** at the same point in a movement. See [Injury Prevention & Pain Rules](19-injury-prevention.md).

Show any coach this chapter and tell them your current gate, and expect them to work within it. In the UK, look for UKSCA-accredited or CIMSPA-registered coaches.

## Your actions this week

- Set up each Phase 1 machine one-handed and note the settings: seat, pads, pins and the leg press's stop hole.
- Practise the brace with five slow breaths in your 90/90 breathing, then breathe out on every leg-press rep.
- Film one set each of rear-foot-elevated split squats and single-leg RDLs from the side, and check that your uninjured hand only touches the support.
- Practise getting down to the floor and up again without your injured arm, three times ([chapter 07](07-mobility.md#getting-to-and-from-the-floor) shows how).
- Ask staff to set the leg press's bottom stops for your dead start, and note the hole number.
