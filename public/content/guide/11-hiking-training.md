# Training for Long-Distance Hiking

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - Hiking is a leg-endurance sport with two faces. **Uphill** tests your engine. **Downhill** tests your brakes: the quads work eccentrically, and knee forces roughly double those of flat walking.
> - You can build most of it **now**, wherever you live, without the injured arm: Zone 2 on the stair climber and incline treadmill, step-ups on a low step, Patrick steps, calves, shins and hips (slow step-downs join in Phase 2a), plus daily walks and a weekend long walk on firm, level ground.
> - Packs follow [the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule): a waist pack (2 kg or less) now, a light day pack from Light use / rehab started (2–4 kg to start), about 10% of your body weight at most until Full clearance, and real loads only after it.
> - Two poles need Grip cleared, 70% grip and your physio's OK. Hill paths and any rough, muddy or slippery ground need Full clearance.
> - Long days are built from time on your feet, food on the move (the one fuelling rule is in [Nutrition](14-nutrition.md)), good foot care and a taper. Until Phase 4 the Saturday walk is capped at 80 minutes; anything longer is an optional extra for a free weekend.

## What hiking actually asks of your body

**Uphill: the engine.** Climbing is concentric work: your glutes, quads and calves shorten under load to lift you, step after step. It's expensive. In a classic treadmill study, walking up a 45% gradient cost about **ten times** the energy of level walking **[Moderate evidence]**. Your engine work is in [chapter 10](10-engine-cardio.md).

**Downhill: the brakes.** Descending is eccentric work: your quads lengthen under load to stop the knee collapsing with every step.
- **Knee force:** peak compressive force at the knee was about **3.9 × bodyweight** in level walking and about **8 × bodyweight** walking downhill. The force behind the kneecap was **3–4 times** greater **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Muscle damage:** in untrained young men, 40 minutes of steep downhill walking cut quad strength by about 19% and caused high soreness. A **5-minute downhill "primer" a week earlier roughly halved the damage**, while a level-walking primer didn't help **[Moderate evidence]**. This "repeated bout effect" is why your programme has slow step-downs long before your first real descent.
- **Balance:** after 30 minutes of simulated steep downhill walking, leg dexterity and balance got worse even though strength hadn't dropped **[Moderate evidence]**. Long descents make you clumsier.

**Ankles and feet.** Uneven ground asks your ankles to react quickly in every direction. In a meta-analysis of seven trials (3,726 people), balance training cut ankle sprains by about **a third** (relative risk 0.65), most clearly in people who had sprained an ankle before **[Strong evidence]**. For you, that training always happens next to a support your uninjured hand can touch.

**Fatigue changes everything.** In Austrian Alpine police data covering more than 7,000 hiking accidents, 69% of the accidents involving women happened on the descent. A recent scoping review lists falls, exhaustion and navigation failures as the main problems for walkers (Tang 2026) **[Moderate evidence]**. For you this is the key idea of the chapter: **a fall late in the day, on tired legs, is your biggest risk.** Every part of your training aims to make the end of the day feel like the start.

| Demand | Train it with | Available |
|---|---|---|
| Uphill engine | Stair climber, incline walking, Zone 2 volume, later hill intervals | **Now** |
| Uphill leg strength | Step-ups, split squats, leg press, calves | **Now** (bodyweight and machines) |
| Downhill brakes | Patrick steps, leg extensions, stair descents with the rail on your uninjured side (slow step-downs from Phase 2a) | **Now** |
| Ankles and balance | Single-leg balance by a support, calf and tibialis work | **Now** |
| Load carriage | Waist pack, then a light day pack (`ruck-light`), then loaded rucking (`ruck-loaded`) | Light use / rehab started → Full clearance |
| Rough-terrain skill | Real terrain, poles and technique ([chapter 12](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md)) | Poles from Grip cleared and 70% grip; terrain from Full clearance |

## Your training week, with or without hills nearby

You don't need hills on your doorstep to train for them **[Expert opinion]**:
- **The gym does the climbing.** The stair climber and incline treadmill give you hundreds of metres of "ascent" a week. Most treadmills barely tilt downhill, so Patrick steps (and slow step-downs from Phase 2a) are your descent training.
- **Everyday walking does the volume.** Daily walks and your commute build towards 8,000–10,000 steps a day. Walk on lit, gritted main routes in the dark months, and skip leafy back streets after rain.
- **Stairs are a free descent primer.** Once a day, walk down one flight slowly with your uninjured hand on the rail. On station stairs, go off-peak, when you won't be jostled, or use the escalator or lift when it's busy.
- **The weekend is your long walk.** In Phases 0–1 it builds from 40 to 80 minutes, on pavements, park tarmac paths or firm, level gravel. This week's route is on the Saturday card and the Hikes page. Use the treadmill (3–8% incline) when it's frosty, icy, leafy and wet, or dark ([chapter 12](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md)).
- **Longer walks are optional extras.** The programme caps the Saturday walk at 80 minutes until Phase 4. On a free weekend, and only on a GREEN day, you can swap it for a longer Level 3 walk on a flat, firm, stile-free route ([chapter 13](13-uk-hiking-roadmap.md)). Add no more than about 15–30 minutes to your longest recent walk, and plan your journey home so you finish an hour before sunset.

## Strength work that transfers

The exercise library holds the set-up, cues and arm-safe instructions for each of these. This is why each one is in your plan. Until Full clearance, every step exercise uses an aerobic step (15–25 cm) or the bottom stair, never a bench.

| Exercise | Why it matters on the hill | Typical dose now | Later progression |
|---|---|---|---|
| `step-up-bw` | The number one hiking exercise: a loaded uphill step | 3 × 8–12 per side on an aerobic step or the bottom stair, uninjured hand on an upright or banister | `db-step-up` (Grip cleared), higher boxes after Full clearance, then step-ups with a pack |
| `lateral-step-up-bw` | Hip stability for cambers and side-steps | 2 × 10 per side, same low step | A higher step after Full clearance |
| `step-down-eccentric` | Downhill braking control, the "repeated bout" primer | From Phase 2a: 3 × 6–8 per side, 3–4 s down | Higher step, then with a pack |
| `patrick-step` | Knee-over-toe strength for steep descents | 2 × 12–15 per side | Add a pack later |
| `split-squat-bw` → `rfe-split-squat-bw` | Single-leg strength and balance | 3 × 8–10 per side, uninjured hand on the rack | `db-split-squat`, `db-bulgarian-split-squat` |
| `leg-press`, `leg-press-single` | Raw leg strength and muscle (the programme's +5 kg goal) | 3 × 8–12 | Heavier |
| `leg-extension` | Quad armour for descents | 2–3 × 12–15, 3 s lowering | Heavier |
| `hamstring-curl` | Knee balance and hamstring strength | 3 × 8–12 | Heavier |
| `calf-raise-single-bw`, `bent-knee-calf-raise` (and `calf-raise-leg-press` from Grip cleared) | Calves and Achilles do the work on every climb | 3 × 12–20 | Slower, then add load |
| `tibialis-raise-wall` | Shins and ankles, for toe control downhill | 2 × 15–25 | Longer sets |
| `side-lying-hip-abduction` | Pelvic stability on uneven ground | 2 × 12–15 per side | Slower, then longer sets |
| `single-leg-rdl-bw`, `glute-bridge-single-leg` | Hamstrings, glutes and balance | 3 × 6–10 per side | Hold a dumbbell (Grip cleared) |
| `single-leg-balance` | Ankle and hip reflexes | 3 × 30 s per side, uninjured hand near a support | Head turns now; eyes closed from Full clearance, always by a support |

How to dose it **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Frequency:** 2–3 strength sessions a week.
- **Effort:** leave 2–3 reps in reserve, and lower slowly on every rep.
- **Two jobs:** heavier sets of 6–12 build strength and muscle, and lighter sets of 15–25+ build muscular endurance. Your programme uses both.
- **Order:** step work comes early in a session, while you're fresh. Fatigued step-ups are where trips happen.

## Muscular endurance: the bridge between gym and hill

Uphill Athlete calls **muscular endurance (ME)** the ability of your legs to keep producing force for hundreds of steps. On steep ground, your legs often fail before your lungs: the quads burn while your breathing feels fine. Their recipe **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Timing:** after a phase of general strength, once a week, for about **8–16 weeks**. They call it "a phase, not a lifestyle".
- **Prerequisite:** your aerobic base comes first.
- **Formats:** steep weighted uphill carries, or gym step-ups and step-downs.
- **Intensity:** the legs burn but you can still talk. If breathing is what stops you, the session is set up wrong.
- **Progression:** shorten the rest between sets before adding load.

Their at-home ME workout builds over about ten sessions to a weight vest of around **20% of bodyweight** (about 16 kg for an 80 kg adult), using step-ups to a box at about 75% of knee height. It also includes jump squats and split jumps. **You don't do the jumps, the vest or the high box until Full clearance.**

### Your arm-safe ME session (optional, from about week 6 of Phase 1)

**This session is optional, and the hub doesn't schedule it.** Phase 1's step-ups and Patrick steps already cover the basics. If you want more hill-specific leg work, use it in place of one Tuesday Zone 2 session a week, only on a GREEN day and never in a deload week, and record it under Log a session as an ad-hoc session **[Expert opinion]**.

1. **Warm-up:** 10 minutes on the bike, then the daily mobility routine.
2. **Step-up blocks:** on an aerobic step (15–25 cm) or the bottom stair, step continuously at a steady rhythm for **5 minutes**, alternating the lead leg every minute, with your uninjured hand on an upright or banister. Rest 2 minutes. Do 3 blocks at first and build to 5.
3. **Descent block:** `patrick-step`, 2 × 12–15 per side, slow and controlled. From Phase 2a, when the programme brings in `step-down-eccentric`, swap to 3 × 8 per side, 3–4 seconds down.
4. **Finish:** 10 minutes of easy incline walking.

Later, the same session becomes "real" ME. From Light use / rehab started you can add the light day pack, within [the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule). From Full clearance you move to higher boxes and heavier packs, then outdoor uphill carries. A favourite is carrying water bottles up a steep hill and **pouring the water out at the top**, so the descent is unloaded and kind to your knees **[Expert opinion]**.

## Stair climber and incline: the numbers

The hub sets your minutes each week ([chapter 04](04-the-program.md)). In Phases 0–1, the stair climber builds from 8 to about 25 minutes and incline walking holds at about 24–30 minutes, at 3–6% for the first few weeks and 6–12% after that (your session notes give the exact figure). All of it is in your Zone 2 (112–131 bpm for the example profile; Settings shows yours), with your uninjured hand resting lightly on the rail, never leaning, and the injured arm across your stomach. Two pieces of treadmill maths:
- **Climb (m) ≈ distance (km) × incline (%) × 10.** So 90 minutes at 4 km/h and 12% (6 km) ≈ **720 m** of climbing, a respectable hill day.
- **The treadmill can't teach you to descend.** That's what Patrick steps, step-downs and slow stair descents are for.

Change one thing at a time (time, incline or speed), let the pace drop on climbs to hold the zone, and if you wobble, slow the machine before you grab anything **[Expert opinion]**. Start and stop the belt while you're standing on it, and never step onto a moving belt ([chapter 10](10-engine-cardio.md)).

## Carrying a pack (rucking): gated

What you carry, on walks and in daily life, follows [the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule) in chapter 18, including how to put a pack on and take it off. In training terms it works out like this:

| Gate and phase | On your long walk | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Now / Good arm OK (Phases 0–1) | **A waist pack only,** 2 kg or less: water, phone, snack and a light layer. No shoulder straps | Firm, level routes (ladder Levels 1–3) |
| Light use / rehab started (still Phase 1) | **A light day pack** (`ruck-light`): 2–4 kg to start, adding about 1 kg every 2–3 weeks if the walk and the next morning feel fine | Firm, level routes (ladder Level 4) |
| Grip cleared (Phases 2–3) | The same progression, carrying on from your Phase 1 weight, up to about 10% of your body weight (8 kg for an 80 kg adult) until Full clearance. In Phase 3, choose good-path routes with more climbing | Good paths only |
| Full clearance (Phases 4–5) | **Loaded rucking** (`ruck-loaded`): from 6–8 kg, adding 1–2 kg every 2 weeks towards **10–15 kg** for multi-day goals | All terrain, one new surface at a time |

**Why so careful?** A meta-analysis found that carrying load measurably **reduces postural stability**, with more sway the heavier and less evenly placed the load. The authors note that falls are a common way load carriers get hurt **[Moderate evidence]**. Getting a rucksack on and off also moves the arm, which is why chapter 18 sets out exactly how to do it: sitting down, uninjured arm first, hip belt fastened.

**How it progresses later:**
- **Easy means easy:** no back, hip, knee or foot pain the next day, and no change in the arm. Change one variable a week: load, distance or ascent.
- **Typical targets:** about **6–8 kg** for a big day hike, **10–12 kg** for long winter days with extra layers, and **12–15 kg** for self-supported multi-day trips. Heavier than that is rarely worth it, so spend money on lighter kit instead **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Frequency:** one loaded session a week is plenty. Military research suggests a heavy load-carriage session every **10–14 days**, combined with strength and aerobic training, is enough. A progressive load-carriage programme in US Marine Corps recruits cut the share injured from 58% to 39% **[Moderate evidence]**.

## Long days and back-to-back days

Long-distance hiking is about **time on your feet**. Build the long day first, then learn to repeat it **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Long day:** add about 15–30 minutes, or 100–200 m of ascent, per week, but not both. Every 4th or 5th week, cut it by about a third. Until Phase 4 the programme holds the planned walk at 80 minutes, so anything longer is an optional free-weekend extra on a GREEN day.
- **Back-to-back days (Phases 4–5):** follow your long Saturday with an easy Sunday on good paths. In the hub it builds from about 45 minutes to 2–3 hours. This teaches you to walk well on tired legs, which is what day two of any multi-day trail feels like.
- **Rehearse:** 4–6 weeks before a multi-day trip, do two or three days in a row with your real pack.
- **Pacing:** the first hour should feel too easy. Hilly ground pulls you into harder zones without you noticing, so on climbs shorten your stride instead of speeding up your breathing.

## Poles: from Grip cleared, 70% grip and your physio's OK

Poles turn hiking into four-limb walking, and the evidence on downhill knee loads is good:
- **Knee forces:** in a lab study on a 25° downhill ramp, poles cut ground reaction force, knee moment and knee joint forces by **12–25%** **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Overall:** reviews agree that poles reduce lower-limb loading, at the price of a little more cardiovascular work **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Muscle damage:** in a small study, poles reduced markers of muscle and cartilage damage after downhill walking **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Falls:** in two studies of descents on Mount Fuji, pole use was linked to a lower fall risk in some groups **[Moderate evidence]**.

**The gate:**
- **Two poles** need two hands that can grip and take some load: Grip cleared, at least 70% grip and your physio's agreement. Then practise on the firm, level routes you're already allowed. Poles don't open up new terrain; hill paths and rough ground still wait for Full clearance.
- **Before then,** you don't need a pole on flat, firm routes. If you'd like one in your uninjured hand, ask the clinic first, and keep the wrist strap off so you can let go in a stumble.

**Technique** **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Length:** elbows at about 90° on the flat, a little shorter uphill, a little longer downhill.
- **Downhill:** plant the poles slightly ahead and step down between them, without reaching so far that the poles take your weight.
- **Straps:** in rough terrain, take your hands out of the wrist straps so a pole can't lever your wrist in a slip. Never use the strap on your injured side.

## Fuelling and hydration on long days

You're not dieting while the bone heals, and big days burn a lot of energy, so fuel them generously ([chapter 14](14-nutrition.md)).

- **Before:** a normal breakfast with carbohydrate and protein 1–3 hours before you start. One position stand suggests about **500 ml** of fluid around 2 hours before exercise **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **During:** follow the one fuelling rule for long walks in [Nutrition](14-nutrition.md): it says when to start eating. It rests on the sports-nutrition position stands: the International Society of Sports Nutrition supports **30–60 g of carbohydrate an hour** on long efforts, little and often **[Strong evidence]**. For a hiker, once you've started eating, that means a few bites at every stop or every half hour.
- **Fluid:** aim to lose no more than **2% of your body weight** in sweat, which is about 1.6 kg for an 80 kg adult **[Strong evidence]**. Weigh yourself on any bathroom or smart scale before and after a long walk: each kilogram lost is roughly a litre of sweat you didn't replace.
- **Don't over-drink.** Drinking far beyond thirst can dilute blood sodium. For outings over an hour, a drink with some sodium (an older position stand suggests 0.5–0.7 g per litre) helps **[Moderate evidence]**.

**Cheap hiker food** (carbohydrate figures are typical, so check the label; the notes flag gelatine if you eat halal or vegetarian):

| Food | Approximate carbohydrate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Banana | about 20–25 g | Pack it on top so it doesn't get squashed |
| Supermarket flapjack or cereal bar | often 20–35 g | Own-brand multipacks are cheap. Bars with marshmallow usually contain gelatine |
| Jelly sweets, 50 g | about 40 g | Most standard jelly sweets contain gelatine, often from pork. If you avoid it, choose vegan or halal-certified ones and check the label |
| Jam or honey sandwich | about 40–50 g | Real food for lunch |
| Malt loaf, 2 slices | about 30–40 g | A classic UK hill snack |
| Dates or raisins | check the label | Dense, cheap fuel that survives a squashed pack |
| Salted crisps or nuts | low carbohydrate, but salt and fat | Good with water on longer days |

Practise in training: your Saturday walks are where you learn what your stomach tolerates. Never try a new food on the big day. In the UK hills, carry enough water to finish the day, because streams may be contaminated by livestock. If you plan to refill, use a filter or purification tablets **[Expert opinion]**.

## Feet: blisters and foot care

The NHS's advice works for hikers too: wear comfortable, well-fitting footwear, break new shoes in **gradually**, and wear moisture-wicking socks, changing them if your feet get sweaty. Don't burst a blister. Cover it with a soft plaster or a **hydrocolloid** dressing, and call NHS 111 if it looks infected (hot, with green or yellow pus or spreading redness). **Paper tape** on blister-prone spots before exercise cut blisters by about **40%** in a large ultramarathon trial, although an earlier, smaller trial found no significant effect **[Moderate evidence]**.

Hiker habits **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Stop at the first hot spot** and tape it there and then. Two minutes now saves your day.
- **Trim your toenails** straight across a couple of days before a long walk.
- **Keep your laces snug for descents,** so your foot doesn't slide forward. One-handed, elastic or lock laces hold the tension for you ([chapter 12](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md)).
- **Carry spare socks** on long days, change them at lunch, and air your feet at long stops.

## Recovery after big days

- **The first two hours:** eat carbohydrate plus **20–40 g of protein**, then rehydrate. The ISSN notes that protein in the 2 hours after exercise strongly stimulates muscle building, and that spreading protein every 3–4 hours across the day works best **[Strong evidence]**.
- **The next day:** 20–40 minutes of easy Zone 1 (walking or the bike) and your mobility routine. Soreness after a big descent usually peaks one to three days later **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Readiness rule:** the next morning's check-in decides the day. Soreness of 7/10 or more, a raised resting HR or under 6 hours' sleep makes it AMBER: halve the sets, Zone 2 only, no intervals, same loads. The full rule is in [chapter 19](19-injury-prevention.md).
- **The arm:** check it after every long day. Any red flag, or arm pain of 7/10 or more, makes it a RED day: no training, including your physio exercises, until you've been advised ([chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md)).

## Tapering before a big hike

A meta-analysis of tapering found the best results came from about **2 weeks**, cutting training volume by **41–60%** while keeping intensity and frequency the same **[Strong evidence]**. For a single big day hike, 7–10 days is usually enough **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Last long hike:** 10–14 days before.
- **Final week:** about half your normal volume, keeping a few short, brisk efforts.
- **Strength:** one light session early in the final week. No new exercises and no heavy eccentric work, because you want fresh quads.
- **Last few days:** sleep, eat normally with a bit more carbohydrate the day before, pack and check your kit, and check the forecast and your transport.

## A sample 12-week "first big hike" block (for later)

**Who it's for:** you, after Full clearance, once you've comfortably completed ladder Level 5 ([chapter 13](13-uk-hiking-roadmap.md)). **The target:** a big hill day of about **15–20 km and 900–1,300 m of ascent** over 7–9 hours, carrying **6–8 kg**. That's the scale of the classic mountain paths up Yr Wyddfa (Snowdon) or Ben Nevis.

> [!NOTE]
> **How this fits your hub programme:** Phases 4–5 ([chapter 04](04-the-program.md)) already follow these principles, with Monday as a rest day, intervals on Wednesday and long days peaking at about 6 hours. Follow the hub day to day, and use this block as the template when you book a specific event.

| Week | Phase | Saturday long hike | Sunday | Pack (% of bodyweight) | ME session | Intervals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Base | 3 h, 400–500 m | Rest | ~7% | 3 × 5 min step-ups | 30/90s × 6 |
| 2 | Base | 3.5 h, 500–600 m | 1 h easy | ~8% | 4 × 5 min | 30/90s × 8 |
| 3 | Build | 4 h, 600–700 m | 1–1.5 h | ~10% | 4 × 5 min with pack | 4 × 3 min |
| 4 | **Deload** | 2.5 h, ~400 m | Rest | ~8% | 2 × 5 min | 30/90s × 6 |
| 5 | Build | 4.5 h, 700–800 m | 1.5–2 h | ~10% | 5 × 5 min | 4 × 4 |
| 6 | Build | 5 h, 800–900 m | 2 h | ~10–12% | 5 × 5 min | 4 × 4 |
| 7 | Build | 5.5 h, 900–1,000 m | 2–2.5 h | target load | 6 × 5 min | Hill repeats 5 × 4 min |
| 8 | **Deload** | 3 h, ~500 m | 1 h | ~8% | 3 × 5 min | 30/90s × 6 |
| 9 | Peak | 6–7 h, 1,000–1,200 m | 2.5–3 h | target load | 6 × 5 min | Hill repeats 6 × 4 min |
| 10 | **Dress rehearsal** | 7 h, about the event's ascent, full kit, real hill terrain | 2 h | target load | 5 × 5 min | 4 × 4 |
| 11 | Taper 1 | 4 h, ~600 m | 1 h | target load | 3 × 5 min, lighter | 4 × 3 min |
| 12 | Taper 2 + **event** | Event day (Sat). Mid-week: 60–90 min easy with the pack | — | target load | None | 30/90s × 4, early in the week |

Block rules **[Expert opinion]**:
- **One variable at a time:** each week, raise time, ascent **or** pack.
- **Repeat a week** if soreness lasts over 48 hours, if you finish a hike unusually wrecked, or at any arm warning sign.
- **Footwear:** boots broken in by week 6, and nothing new on your feet after week 9.
- **Don't train through bad weather.** Mountain forecasts and wind limits ([chapter 12](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md)) apply to training hikes too. A treadmill "hike simulation" is a fine stand-in.
- **If the hills are far from home,** big days mean early starts or a weekend away. Book well ahead, and on public transport, check the last train or bus back before you set a turn-around time.
