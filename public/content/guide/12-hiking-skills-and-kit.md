# Hiking Skills, Kit & Mountain Safety

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - **Kit on a budget:** buy grippy shoes, easy laces, a waist pack, a head torch and a basic waterproof first. Everything else waits for the gate that needs it.
> - **Winter walking:** walk in daylight (midwinter sunset is around 16:00 in southern England, and earlier further north), use the treadmill on frosty or wet-leaf mornings, and keep well away from towpath edges.
> - **Until Full clearance:** tarmac or firm, level gravel only, dry and stile-free (ladder Levels 1–4), with your uninjured hand always free. Packs follow [the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule): a waist pack (2 kg or less) until Light use / rehab started. A fall onto the injured arm is the thing we're avoiding.
> - **Before every walk:** ask AdventureSmart's three questions and text someone a route card with your travel plans. In an emergency, call **999 or 112**. On the hills, ask for **Police**, then **Mountain Rescue**. Register for **emergencySMS** now.
> - **For hill days later:** a paper OS map and compass you can use, the Met Office mountain forecast or MWIS, and a turn-around time.

## Before you go: the three questions

AdventureSmart UK, the national outdoor safety campaign, boils planning down to three questions. If you can't answer "yes" to all three, change the plan.

1. "Am I confident I have the **knowledge and skills** for the day?"
2. "Do I know what the **weather** will be like?"
3. "Do I have the right **gear**?"

Two more of their messages are worth learning by heart: "If things don't go to plan, turn around and follow the path back", and shortening or postponing a walk is a sensible choice, not a failure **[Expert opinion]**.

## Kit on a budget: what to buy first

The hub's *Hikes* page has the full kit list. The order below spends money only when a gate makes the kit useful. Shop names are examples, not endorsements, and prices and ranges change (not verified) **[Expert opinion]**.

| When | Buy | Why, and where to save |
|---|---|---|
| **Now** | Walking or trail shoes with deep, grippy lugs | Grip is your main safety feature on wet pavements and leaves. Spend your money here, and try them on in person |
| **Now** | Elastic or lock laces | One-handed lacing, and no loose laces |
| **Now** | A waist pack (hip pack) | Water, phone, snack and a layer, 2 kg or less, with your uninjured hand free and no shoulder straps |
| **Now** | A rechargeable head torch | Dark by about 16:30 in midwinter, and earlier further north. Budget ones are fine |
| **Now** | 2 pairs of merino or synthetic walking socks | Never cotton. Blends cost less than pure merino |
| **Now** | Hat, gloves and an easy-on mitt for the injured hand | Cold often makes a healing arm ache (commonly reported, not verified). A loose mitt slips on over a stiff hand |
| **Now** | A basic hooded waterproof jacket with taped seams, and loose sleeves | Budget versions are fine for low-level walks. Big zip pulls are easier one-handed |
| **Now** | Blister plasters (hydrocolloid) and paper tape | Pharmacy or supermarket own-brand |
| **Light use / rehab started** | A 20–30 litre day pack with a padded hip belt, plus a dry bag or bin-bag liner | Try it on in a shop with some weight in it, and practise putting it on sitting down, uninjured arm first ([the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule)) |
| **Grip cleared, 70% grip, physio OK** | Trekking poles | Budget poles do the job |
| **Before your first hill day (Full clearance)** | Walking boots, waterproof over-trousers with long side zips, an insulated jacket, an OS map and compass, a whistle, a survival bag and a first aid kit | Break the boots in over several flat walks first |

**Where the money goes furthest:**
- **Budget outdoor chains,** such as Decathlon, Mountain Warehouse, Go Outdoors and Millets, and Sports Direct's Karrimor range, cover almost everything on this list. Their sales are frequent.
- **Discount shops and supermarket specials,** such as TK Maxx and the Aldi or Lidl middle aisles, sometimes have base layers, socks and head torches.
- **Second-hand** (eBay, Vinted, Facebook Marketplace and charity shops) is great for fleeces, insulated jackets and packs. Check that the seams and zips work, and never buy second-hand footwear, because it's moulded to someone else's feet.
- **Don't skimp on** the grip of your shoes, a waterproof that keeps you dry, or a head torch that works.

## Footwear: flat, firm walks now, hills later

| Stage | What to wear | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Now** (pavements, tarmac and firm, level gravel) | Trail shoes or walking shoes with **deep lugs and soft, grippy rubber**, a firm heel and room for thick socks | Light and comfortable. You need grip, not ankle support. Waterproofing is optional on your dry, firm routes, but grip on wet leaves isn't |
| **Later** (Full clearance: rough, wet and rocky ground) | Walking boots, usually mid-cut and waterproof, with a stiffer sole | Protection from rocks and bog, and better edging on steep ground |

Fit and breaking in **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Shop late in the day,** when your feet are at their largest, and wear your hiking socks.
- **Check the fit.** You want a thumb's width of room in front of your toes, and your heel shouldn't lift. On the shop's slope, your toes shouldn't touch the front when you walk down.
- **Break them in gradually.** The NHS's blister advice says the same. Do 3–4 short walks, then a half-day, before any long day.
- **One-handed lacing** is the hard part. Elastic or lock laces, or boots with lace-locking hooks, solve it. Until then, ask for help, and never walk with loose laces.
- **Bin anything with a smooth, worn sole,** whatever it cost.

## Clothing and packs

You adjust layers instead of sweating, then freezing. AdventureSmart's list, in order: a breathable synthetic or merino **base layer** ("avoid cotton t-shirts"), a fleece or soft-shell **mid layer**, an **insulated jacket** for stops (synthetic stays warmer when damp), a **waterproof jacket** with a good hood ("don't leave home without it"), **waterproof over-trousers**, quick-drying **walking trousers** ("avoid denim"), and a **hat and gloves**, which Mountain Rescue says to carry all year.

For the healing arm **[Expert opinion]**:
- Choose loose sleeves that fit over any splint or brace, and big zip pulls.
- Put the injured arm into sleeves first, and take it out last.
- Put over-trousers on **sitting down**, never hopping on one leg.
- Don't strap anything tight around the injured forearm unless the clinic is happy with it.

**Packs:** a waist pack now (2 kg or less), a 20–30 litre day pack with a hip belt from Light use / rehab started, and a fitted 40–60 litre pack for multi-day trips much later. What goes in them, and how to put a pack on and take it off, follows [the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule). Rucksack fabric isn't waterproof at the seams, so line it with a dry bag or a strong bin bag.

## Winter walking

If you start the programme in autumn or winter, these are the conditions you'll train in for the first few months.

**Daylight.** Around the shortest day, southern England gets under 8 hours of daylight, with sunset around 16:00 and civil twilight (when it's still light enough to walk) over by about 16:30. Further north the days are shorter still. Look up sunrise, sunset and civil twilight for where you live on timeanddate.com or your phone's weather app.

**Your winter rules** **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Dark mornings are gym time.** In midwinter it's dark until after 07:00 in southern England, and later further north, so early sessions belong on the treadmill and stair climber, not on unlit pavements.
- **Walk outdoors in the middle of the day,** and plan weekend walks to finish an hour before sunset: around 15:00 in southern England in midwinter. If you're travelling by train or bus, count back from your journey home.
- **Wet leaves are as slippery as ice,** and they hide kerbs, potholes and roots. Keep to the cleared middle of the pavement, and slow down on slopes, ramps and bridges.
- **Ice comes first in predictable places** after a clear, cold night: bridges and footbridges, metal grates and covers, painted lines, wooden decking and boardwalks, and shaded paths. If the pavement glistens, assume it's ice. On frosty mornings, the treadmill wins.
- **Wet stone and station entrances** get slippery in rain. Take polished steps and tiled floors slowly, with your uninjured hand on the rail.
- **Carry a head torch** on every walk, and wear something bright on roads after dusk.

**Towpaths: keep away from the edge.** Canal towpaths are flat and tempting, but many are narrow, unfenced and unlit, with mooring rings, ropes, cobbles and low bridges. The Canal & River Trust's towpath code says "pedestrians have priority" and "cyclists must slow down for others", and the Trust warns that cold water can cause cold shock and hypothermia. A one-handed fall into a canal in winter is far worse than one on a pavement. Until Full clearance **[Expert opinion]**:
- Use a towpath only if it's surfaced, dry and wide, and in daylight. Never use one in ice, after dark or when it's flooded.
- Walk on the **inside, away from the water**, and keep your uninjured hand free.
- **When bikes pass, step to the inside and stop** rather than walking on, with the injured arm across your body.
- Treat lock-sides, cobbles, mooring rings and ramps under bridges as hazards, and walk them slowly.

## Fall prevention with a healing arm

This is the most important section in this chapter.

**Why the risk is real:**
- In Austrian Alpine police data, 69% of women's hiking accidents happened on the descent **[Moderate evidence]**.
- After 30 minutes of steep downhill walking, balance and leg dexterity got worse even without strength loss **[Moderate evidence]**.
- Carrying load measurably increases sway **[Moderate evidence]**.
- Falls cluster late in the day, on descents, in poor conditions, and when you're tired, rushed or distracted **[Expert opinion]**.

**Your rules until Full clearance** **[Expert opinion]**:
1. **Terrain:** only tarmac or firm, level gravel, dry and stile-free: ladder Levels 1–3, plus Level 4 with a light pack from Light use / rehab started. Turn back if the surface turns muddy, loose, stepped or steep. Example routes are in [chapter 13](13-uk-hiking-roadmap.md).
2. **Conditions:** no frost or ice, no wet leaves, mossy boardwalks or polished stone, and daylight only. Dusk counts as dark.
3. **Your uninjured hand stays free.** Use a waist pack (2 kg or less until Light use / rehab started), keep your hands out of your pockets, and carry nothing, not even a coffee or your phone. Stop walking to look at your map or screen.
4. **Grip underfoot:** lugged soles in good condition.
5. **Pace:** short steps on slopes, kerbs and ramps. Never hurry for a train or bus, because there's always another one.
6. **Fatigue:** turn back while you still feel fresh, and plan routes so the last 30 minutes are flat and easy, ideally ending close to your way home.
7. **People, bikes and dogs:** on shared paths, walk to one side and be predictable. When someone passes close, keep the injured arm across your body.
8. **If you start to slip:** bend your knees, get low and sit down. Land on your bottom or your side, not your hands, with the injured arm tucked across your body. Don't practise falling. Just make sitting down your reflex.
9. **Arm protection:** wear any splint or brace the clinic gave you for outdoor use, exactly as instructed.

**After Full clearance:** open up the terrain list **one row at a time**. Walk each new surface on a short, easy route first, with poles, ideally with a qualified leader or on a course.

## Route cards and telling someone

Mountaineering Scotland's minimum is to tell a trusted person where you're going and your route, when you expect to be back, where you've parked (or which trains or buses you're taking), what you're wearing, and your mobile number. Make sure they know **what to do if you don't check in**, and tell them when you're back so there's no false alarm. If you walk alone, stick to the route you left, and let your contact know if plans change.

An example card to text the night before. Fill in the brackets:

```
Sat [date] — [your name], mobile [your number]
Route: [start] → [trail] → [finish] (Level 2–3, flat and firm)
Getting there: [train, bus or where I've parked, and the time]. Planned way home: [time] from [station or car park].
Turn-around: [time] wherever I am. Sunset [time].
Wearing: [jacket colour], [trousers], [pack colour].
If no text from me by [time]: call me twice, then call 999, ask for Police,
and give them this message.
```

## In an emergency

Mountain Rescue England and Wales:
1. **Dial 999 or 112.**
2. Ask for **"Police"**, then, on the hills, **"Mountain Rescue"**.
3. **Give these details:**
  - your location (a grid reference if possible)
  - the casualty's name, gender and age
  - the nature of the injuries or emergency
  - the number of people in your party
  - your mobile number
4. **Stay where you are** until the rescue team contacts you.

**emergencySMS.** Register **before** you need it: text "register" to 999 and follow the instructions. In an emergency, you can then text 999 with "Police", the details and the location.

**Signal for help.** Give six long blasts on a whistle, or six torch flashes, in a minute, then pause for a minute and repeat.

**Keep warm while you wait.** Put on every layer, get into a survival bag, and sit on your pack. The NHS lists the signs of hypothermia as shivering, pale cold skin, slurred speech, slow breathing, tiredness or confusion. Move the person to shelter, replace wet clothing, insulate them (including the head), and give warm, non-alcoholic drinks and sugary food if they're fully awake.

> [!SAFETY]
> **If you fall onto the injured arm:**
> - **Steady it first:** support it with your other hand or an improvised sling, such as a buff or a jacket.
> - **Check for red flags:** severe or increasing pain, a change in shape, numbness or tingling, a cold or discoloured hand, or the wound reopening.
> - **If it's serious, or you can't get home safely,** call 999.
> - **Otherwise,** head home carefully and get it checked the same day: fracture clinic, NHS 111 or A&E.
> - **Tick the "fall, knock or jolt onto the arm" red flag** in your check-in. The day turns RED: no training, including your physio exercises, until you've been advised ([chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md)).

## Navigation (for hill days later)

On flat, waymarked routes near towns your phone is enough, as long as it's charged and you have a power bank. On the hills it isn't **[Expert opinion]**. Mountaineering Scotland: "Regardless of which device you choose you should still carry and be able to read a map." A phone "can't read and interpret the map for you".

| Map | Scale | 1 km is… | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **OS Explorer** (orange) | 1:25,000 | 4 cm | Walking. It shows field boundaries, paths and fine detail |
| **OS Landranger** (pink) | 1:50,000 | 2 cm | Planning, and longer routes |

**Five skills to learn first:**
1. **Setting the map,** so the features on it line up with what you see.
2. **Grid references:** 4 figures name a 1 km square, and 6 figures narrow it to 100 m. Eastings first ("along the corridor, then up the stairs").
3. **Contours:** lines close together mean steep ground.
4. **Handrails and catching features:** follow walls, streams and paths, and know the feature that tells you you've gone too far.
5. **Timing:** Naismith's rule works out at about **5 km/h plus 10 minutes per 100 m of climbing**. In a study of about 88,000 km of UK walking tracks, rules like this were within about 13–15% for whole routes on average **[Moderate evidence]**. Until you know your own pace on hill ground, add 25–50% **[Expert opinion]**.

**Phone tips** (Mountaineering Scotland): download offline maps (OS or Harvey) beforehand, use flight mode to save battery, keep the phone warm in an inside pocket, use a waterproof case, and carry a power bank and cable. Taking a bearing in poor visibility is best learnt in person.

## Weather (for hill days later)

| Service | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| [Met Office mountain forecasts](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/specialist-forecasts/mountain) | Ten UK upland regions, issued before 6pm daily for the next two days, plus a 3-day outlook |
| [MWIS](https://www.mwis.org.uk/) | Forecasts for 10 upland regions, written "largely in the form of warnings" |
| [Lake District Weatherline](https://lakedistrict.gov.uk/explore/plan-your-visit/lake-district-weather/) | In winter, Fell Top Assessors report from Helvellyn |
| [SAIS](https://www.sais.gov.uk/) | Daily winter avalanche forecasts for six Scottish areas |

**Wind** is the hill walker's main enemy. Mountaineering Scotland's guide: under 20 mph is negligible, 20–30 mph brings real wind chill, and 30–40 mph "starts to affect the balance of a fit, strong adult". From 50 mph it's "VERY challenging and exhausting", and above 60 mph there's a high risk of being blown over. 0 °C in a 20–30 mph wind feels like about −10 °C.

**Your rules** **[Expert opinion]:** until Full clearance, if gusts over 30 mph are forecast, walk somewhere sheltered and low-level instead. After clearance, 40 mph+ gusts on exposed ground mean a different, lower route.

## The Countryside Code

The Countryside Code for England and Wales has four parts: **respect everyone** (leave gates as you find them), **protect the environment** (take your litter home), **enjoy the outdoors** (check your route and conditions), and **know the signs and symbols**:

| Waymark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Yellow arrow | Footpath |
| Blue arrow | Bridleway (walkers, cyclists and horses) |
| Purple arrow | Restricted byway |
| Red arrow | Byway open to all traffic |
| Acorn | National Trail |

In Scotland, the Scottish Outdoor Access Code rests on three principles: "Respect the interests of others", "Care for the environment" and "Take responsibility for your own actions". In the stalking season, check Heading for the Scottish Hills before walking in deer forest.

## Terrain technique

This is when each surface opens up for you. The hub's *Hikes* page has the same list as quick-reference cards.

| Terrain | Gate | Core technique |
|---|---|---|
| Tarmac and firm, level gravel | Now (Levels 1–4; Level 4 with Light use / rehab started) | Eyes 2–3 steps ahead, wary of wet leaves, boardwalks, metal grates and ice. Turn back if it turns muddy, loose, stepped or steep |
| Well-made hill paths (dry, moderate gradients) | Full clearance | Steady pace uphill. Shorter steps, soft knees and poles planted ahead on the way down |
| Stiles and walls | Full clearance | Until then, choose stile-free routes |
| Wet grass and muddy slopes | Full clearance | Flat-footed steps, zig-zag down, use the edges of the path |
| Mud, bog and peat | Full clearance | Read the vegetation: bright green moss and rushes mean wet. Test with a pole |
| Stone-pitched paths and rock steps | Full clearance | Step **onto** the stones, not between them. Slow down on wet rock |
| Boulders, rocky ground and limestone pavement | Full clearance | Three points of contact on awkward moves. Never jump between boulders |
| Scree | Full clearance | Avoid it where a path exists, and go down in short, controlled steps |
| Scrambling (Grades 1–3) | Full clearance + a course | Face in on steep sections, test your holds, know your escape routes |
| Streams and river crossings | Full clearance | Avoid them: wait, or choose a bridge |
| Snow and ice | Full clearance + a winter skills course | Ice axe and crampon skills. An ice-axe self-arrest loads both wrists hard |
| Steep descents and exposed ridges | Full clearance | Short steps, weight over your feet, turn back in wind |

**Descending** **[Expert opinion]:** shorten your stride and keep your knees soft, keep your weight over your feet (leaning back is how feet shoot out on grass), zig-zag down steep grass, sit and slide off big steps, and slow down in the last hour, when tired legs make mistakes.

**Scrambling** is graded 1 to 3 in the UK, and exposure matters as much as difficulty **[Expert opinion]**. Striding Edge on Helvellyn is "easy but exposed scrambling". Crib Goch on Yr Wyddfa is, in the National Park's words, "a 500-metre knife-edge ridge with no escape route" that "should not be attempted by novice walkers". You need two hands you trust completely, so scrambling waits for Full clearance and well beyond.
