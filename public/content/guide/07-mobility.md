# Mobility & Movement Quality

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - Ten minutes a day (`mobility-daily`) keeps your ankles, hips and upper back moving well for hills, stairs and long days on your feet.
> - The routine runs standing drills first, then **one** trip to the floor: down via a bench with your uninjured hand on it, injured arm across your stomach, and back up through a half-kneel for the last stretch.
> - This chapter is the home of **getting to and from the floor**. Everything happens on your uninjured side, and you never lean on, push through or lie on the injured arm.
> - The last stretch is the half-kneeling hip-flexor stretch until you set Light use / rehab started. The couch stretch unlocks at that gate.
> - Mobility gives you usable range and control, but strength and balance training prevent far more injuries than stretching does. Your wrist and forearm belong to your physio.

## Why mobility matters for your goals

Hiking is thousands of single-leg steps on slopes, steps and uneven ground. Three areas do most of the moving:
- **Ankles (dorsiflexion: the shin moving forward over the foot).** Uphill steps, stairs and descents all need it. When the ankle runs out of range, the knee and foot make up the difference, usually by caving in or turning out. In 1,093 army recruits, limited ankle dorsiflexion predicted injury, while calf stretching before exercise showed no clear protective effect (Pope 1998) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Hips (extension).** Letting the leg trail behind you gives a powerful, efficient stride and a strong push uphill. Long hours of sitting tend to stiffen the front of the hip **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Upper back (rotation).** Your upper back turns with every step and carries a rucksack's load. People who guard an injured arm often stiffen through the upper back and shoulders **[Expert opinion]**.

**What mobility can and can't do.** Stretching on its own barely changes injury rates. In a meta-analysis of randomised trials, the risk ratio for stretching was 0.96 (essentially no effect), compared with 0.32 for strength training (Lauersen 2014) **[Strong evidence]**. Strength training improves range of motion about as much as stretching does (Afonso 2021) **[Moderate evidence]**. So your routine has three jobs: keep the range you need, practise *controlling* it, and give every day a calm, consistent start. Strength and balance work does the heavy lifting for injury prevention ([chapter 19](19-injury-prevention.md)).

## The arm rules for every drill

> [!SAFETY]
> - **No weight through the injured hand, wrist or forearm.** No hands-on-the-floor positions, no all-fours work, and no propping yourself up on that arm.
> - **The injured arm rests** across your stomach, on your thigh, or in a sling if your team still wants one. It never helps you balance.
> - **Balance drills happen within instant reach of a support** for your uninjured hand.
> - **Breathe throughout.** No breath-holding, and never force a stretch beyond about 6/10.
> - **Stop** if the arm aches more, throbs, swells, tingles or goes numb. Tick the matching red flag if it doesn't settle ([chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md)).

## Getting to and from the floor

This is the one method for every floor transition in the hub: the mobility routine, floor exercises in your sessions, and getting up after anything else. Use it every time, and practise it twice this week before you need it **[Expert opinion]**. When a session has several floor exercises, do them back to back, so you get down once and up once.

**Set-up:** lay a mat beside a flat bench, so that when you kneel on the mat the bench is on your **uninjured side**, within easy reach. At home, a sturdy chair or the edge of the bed does the same job.

**Getting down:**
1. Stand beside the bench with your uninjured hand on it and your injured arm across your stomach.
2. Step back with the foot on your injured side and lower that knee onto the mat, so you're half-kneeling with the foot on your uninjured side forward.
3. Bring the other knee down next to it, so you're kneeling upright.
4. Sit down towards your uninjured side: lower that hip onto the mat, moving your uninjured hand from the bench to the mat as you go.
5. **To lie on your back:** lower yourself onto your uninjured forearm, then roll onto your back. The injured arm stays across your stomach.

**Getting up:**
1. Bend your knees and roll onto your **uninjured** side. Your injured arm rests on top, along that side of your body.
2. Push up with your uninjured hand until you're sitting on that hip.
3. Come up onto both knees. If the bench isn't on your uninjured side, shuffle round on your knees until it is.
4. Put your uninjured hand on the bench, step one foot forward into a half-kneel, and stand up by driving through that front leg. In the daily routine, this half-kneel is where drill 8 happens, so you stretch on the way up.

**Which side to lie on:** your back or your uninjured side. None of these drills needs you to lie on your injured side, so don't, and never roll over onto or trap the injured arm. If a programme exercise would have you lying on your injured side, its exercise library entry explains how to protect the forearm or gives a standing version.

**If a floor transition feels wobbly,** or the arm complains, use the no-floor alternatives for drills 6 and 7, and do drill 8 standing in a long split stance with your uninjured hand on the bench. They do the same job.

## The daily routine (`mobility-daily`, about 10 minutes)

The programme and this chapter use exactly the same routine. Drills 1–5 are standing or on a bench. Then you get down once for drills 6 and 7, and do drill 8 on the way back up.

| Order | Drill | Dose | What it's for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `ankle-knee-to-wall` | 10 / side | Ankle dorsiflexion for hills and stairs |
| 2 | `hip-circles-standing` | 5 each way / side | Hip control through its full circle |
| 3 | `single-leg-balance` | 30 s / side (uninjured hand by a support) | Ankle and hip stability for uneven ground |
| 4 | `deep-squat-hold-box` | 60 s | Hips, knees and ankles together |
| 5 | `thoracic-rotation-seated` | 8 / side, on a bench | Upper-back rotation |
| 6 (floor) | `hip-90-90-switch` | 8 switches | Hip rotation in both directions |
| 7 (floor) | `breathing-90-90` | 5 slow breaths | Rib position, bracing and calm |
| 8 | `hip-flexor-stretch-half-kneeling` | 45 s / side, by a bench, with your uninjured hand on it. **From Light use / rehab started, the couch stretch (`couch-stretch`) is an optional progression** | Front of the hip and thigh, for hip extension |

**When the hub schedules it:** on strength days it's part of the warm-up (`warmup-strength`). On cardio, interval, long-walk and rest days it comes after the session, so you do it once every day.

### 1. Knee-to-wall ankle rocks — 10 / side

**Set-up:** face a wall with your uninjured hand flat on it at chest height and your injured arm across your stomach. Take a short split stance with the front toes about 5–10 cm from the wall. Facing the wall works for both legs, so your uninjured hand never has to move.

**Cues:**
- Keep the front heel heavy and on the floor the whole time.
- Drive the knee forward over your second and third toes until it touches, or nearly touches, the wall.
- Move smoothly, about 1 second in and 1 second back, without bouncing.
- When the touch gets easy, slide the foot back 1 cm.

**Check it** with the knee-to-wall test (Progress → Tests): barefoot, measuring from your big toe to the wall, best of 3 per side. In one study of 50 adults the average was about 10 cm (Hall & Docherty 2017). Changes smaller than about 2 cm can be measurement noise (Powden 2015) **[Moderate evidence]**. A gap of more than about 2 cm between sides is worth working on **[Expert opinion]**.

### 2. Standing hip circles — 5 each way / side

**Set-up:** face a wall or rack upright at arm's length, uninjured hand resting on it, injured arm across your stomach. Facing the support works for both legs.

**Cues:**
- Stand tall on one leg with a soft knee, and lift the other knee to hip height.
- Draw the biggest slow circle you can: knee out to the side, round behind you and back down. Then reverse.
- Keep your pelvis and trunk still, so the movement comes from the hip. Put the foot down whenever you wobble.

### 3. Single-leg balance — 30 s / side

**Set-up:** stay facing the wall at arm's length with the fingertips of your uninjured hand touching it, on a firm, flat, non-slip floor in trainers. Injured arm across your stomach. Lift one foot a few centimetres, with a soft standing knee.

**Cues:**
- Spread your toes, keep your weight over the middle of your foot, and fix your eyes on a spot ahead.
- Touch down whenever you need to. The wobbles are the training.

**Progressions now:** move the hand from touching the wall to hovering 2–3 cm away, then add slow head turns, then light toe taps forward and to the side. **Later:** eyes-closed work and unstable surfaces (wobble boards, foam pads) wait for Full clearance. The eyes-open balance test (Progress → Tests) tracks your progress.

### 4. Deep squat hold to a box — 60 s

**Set-up:**
- Put a low box or step (about 20–30 cm) behind you, next to a rack upright on your uninjured side.
- Feet shoulder-width or a little wider, toes turned out 15–30°.
- Hold the upright lightly with your uninjured hand at chest height. Rest the injured arm on your thigh.

**Cues:**
- Sit down between your heels as deep as is comfortable, with the box there to catch you. Losing balance just ends in a sit.
- Keep your knees out over your toes and your weight over the middle of your feet, and breathe slowly into your belly.
- If you can't get near the box, or your heels lift, use a higher box or the bench, and lower it over the weeks.
- Two or three shorter holds that add up to 60 seconds are fine.

**Arm safety:** stand up by driving through your legs, with your uninjured hand on the upright. Never push off your knee with the injured hand.

### 5. Seated thoracic rotation — 8 / side, on a bench

**Set-up:** sit astride a bench, feet flat and knees squeezing the bench so your hips stay still. Rest your injured forearm across your stomach and lay your other forearm over it, so both arms turn with your trunk rather than swinging.

**Cues:**
- Sit tall. Breathe in, then as you breathe out, turn your ribcage to one side, leading with your breastbone and letting your head follow.
- Pause for 2 seconds, come back to the centre, then turn the other way.

**Arm safety:** no floor "open book" rotations until Full clearance, because getting into them means propping yourself up on your hands.

### 6. 90/90 hip switches — 8 switches (floor)

**Set-up:** from the bench, get down with the method above, towards your uninjured side. Sit on the mat with your knees bent to about 90° and your feet flat, wider than your hips. Put your uninjured hand on the mat behind that hip and rest your injured arm in your lap.

**Cues:**
- Let both knees fall slowly to one side, like windscreen wipers, so you land in a 90/90 shape: one shin across in front, the other out to the side. Then switch to the other side. That's 4 each way.
- Breathe out as the knees land, and sit a little taller over the weeks.
- If you feel yourself tipping towards your injured side, make the movement smaller. Never reach out with the injured hand.

**No-floor alternative:** sit on the end of a bench with your feet wide, and let both knees drop to one side and then the other, keeping your chest tall.

### 7. 90/90 breathing — 5 slow breaths (floor)

**Set-up:** lie on your back (step 5 of getting down), with your calves resting on the bench or your feet flat on a wall, so your hips and knees are at about 90°. Rest the injured arm across your stomach, or by your side on a folded towel. Put your uninjured hand on your lower ribs.

**Cues:**
- Gently press your heels into the bench or wall without lifting your hips. You should feel the backs of your thighs switch on and your lower back settle.
- Breathe in through your nose for about 4 seconds, filling your sides and back.
- Breathe out slowly through pursed lips for 6–8 seconds, until your ribs drop and your belly firms. Pause for 2 seconds.
- If you feel light-headed, breathe normally and come up slowly via your uninjured side.

**No-floor alternative:** sit tall on a bench, feet flat, uninjured hand on your lower ribs, and use the same breathing pattern.

### 8. Hip-flexor stretch on the way up — 45 s / side

**Until Light use / rehab started: the half-kneeling hip-flexor stretch (`hip-flexor-stretch-half-kneeling`).** It stretches the same muscles as the couch stretch, but getting in and out is simpler: there's no foot up on a bench behind you, so there's less chance of losing your balance and catching yourself with the injured hand **[Expert opinion]**.
- **Set-up:** come up from the floor to kneeling (getting up, steps 1–3), with the bench on your uninjured side and a pad under your knees. Put your uninjured hand on the bench and step one foot forward, so its knee is over the ankle. Injured arm resting on your front thigh.
- **Cues:** squeeze the glute of the back leg and tuck your tailbone under, as if zipping up tight jeans, until you feel the stretch at the front of that hip. Keep your ribs down, aim for a 5–6/10 stretch, and breathe slowly.
- **Other side:** bring the front knee back down beside the other, then step the other foot forward. Face the same way for both legs, so the bench stays on your uninjured side.
- **Getting out:** stand up from the half-kneel by driving through the front leg, with your uninjured hand on the bench.

**From Light use / rehab started: the couch stretch (`couch-stretch`).** It reaches further into hip extension, so it's the progression once that gate is set and light use of the arm is allowed.
- **Set-up:** kneel on a thick folded mat at the end of a flat bench, with a rack upright (or a second bench) on your **uninjured side** within reach. With your uninjured hand on the upright, rest the top of one foot (laces down) on the bench behind you, then step the other foot forward so its knee is over the ankle. Injured arm across your stomach. Face the same way for both legs, so the support stays on your uninjured side.
- **Cues:** the same glute squeeze and tailbone tuck. **Easier:** lean your torso forward, or move the back knee further from the bench. **Harder:** bring your torso upright.
- **Getting out:** lean forward over the front leg, slide the back foot off the bench, then stand up through a half-kneel with your uninjured hand on the upright.

## Before and after sessions

| Day | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Strength (`warmup-strength`) | 5 min easy on the upright bike, then `mobility-daily`, then 2 ramp-up sets of your first exercise. When the leg press comes first, the ramp-up is 10 easy box squats, so the plates don't change | From Phase 2a, `cooldown-strength`: half-kneeling hip-flexor, seated hamstring and standing calf stretches, so you rise once and never need your injured arm |
| Cardio (`warmup-cardio`, about 4 min) | Leg swings with your uninjured hand on a support, standing hip circles, knee-to-wall rocks | `mobility-daily` |
| Intervals, long walks and rest days | The session's own easy start | `mobility-daily` |

**Mornings: warm up before you bend.** If you train first thing, get warm before any deep or loaded forward bending, such as toe-touches and long hamstring stretches. In people with chronic back pain, avoiding forward bending early in the morning reduced pain (Snook 1998) **[Moderate evidence]**.

**Short holds before, longer holds after.** Static stretches held for less than 60 seconds per muscle barely affect performance, while longer holds reduce it slightly. Dynamic movement before training gives a small boost (Behm 2016) **[Moderate evidence]**. Every hold in the daily routine is 60 seconds or less.

## The post-walk routine (10–15 minutes)

Long walks, especially downhill, leave your calves, quads and feet tired. This routine helps you feel better the next day **[Expert opinion]**:

1. **Walk easy for the last 5 minutes.** Don't stop dead, and don't run for the bus or train.
2. **At home, calf stretch** (`calf-stretch-wall`): 2 × 30 s per side, once with the knee straight and once bent, uninjured hand on the wall.
3. **Hip-flexor stretch** (`hip-flexor-stretch-half-kneeling`): 2 × 30 s per side, kneeling and standing with your uninjured hand on a bench or chair. Or do it standing, in a long split stance.
4. **Hamstring stretch** (`hamstring-stretch-bench`): 30 s per side, sitting with your heel on a bench, spine long.
5. **90/90 breathing:** 5 slow breaths. The seated version is fine.
6. **Foot check.** Look for hot spots and blisters, dress them early, and change into dry socks ([chapter 11](11-hiking-training.md)).
7. **Arm check.** Look for swelling, colour or temperature changes, or anything else on the red-flag list. If your hand has puffed up from hanging by your side all day, rest it raised for a while. If it happens often, mention it to your physio.
8. **Next morning:** repeat the knee-to-wall rock. If one side has lost more than about 2 cm, keep the day easy.

Sore thighs after your first long descents are normal, even for well-trained legs, because steep downhill walking is a different eccentric load from gym work. They ease as you adapt: the repeated-bout effect. Trekking poles reduce this muscle damage (Howatson 2011) **[Moderate evidence]**, but they need grip. Two poles wait for Grip cleared, 70% grip and your physio's OK, and one pole in your uninjured hand needs the clinic's yes ([chapter 11](11-hiking-training.md)).

## Your wrist and forearm: later, and physio-led

Don't stretch, mobilise or "test" the injured wrist or forearm on your own. When your physio prescribes exercises, enter them under *Your physio exercises* on the *Your arm* page. Once they're there and Light use / rehab started is set (the decision table in [chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md) says when), the hub adds one daily item, "Your physio's programme": exactly what your physio prescribed. The `rehab-*` entries in the exercise library, such as `rehab-wrist-flex-ext` and `rehab-forearm-rotation`, are for reference only.

What physios commonly emphasise **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Little and often.** Several short sessions a day usually beat one long, forceful one.
- **Gentle end-range work.** Hold comfortable end positions without forcing them. Don't push the injured hand into range with your other hand unless your physio shows you how.
- **The arm pain rule:** follow your physio's limit. If you weren't given one, keep pain at 2/10 or below during the exercise, back to your usual level by the next morning. Never train through pain that's increasing.
- **Rotation gets special attention.** Turning the palm up and down is often the slowest movement to come back. Even years after plating, people average about 90% of the other side's rotation range (Droll 2007; see [chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md)) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Measure and log.** Record your physio's wrist and rotation figures on the *Your arm* page so you can see the trend.

After Full clearance, mobility grows to include wrist preparation for push-ups and planks, floor positions such as all-fours rotations, and hanging for shoulder and grip health once your grip reaches 90% of the good hand. Each one is added gradually, as your physio advises.
