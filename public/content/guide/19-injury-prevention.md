# Injury Prevention & Pain Rules

> [!NOTE]
> **In short**
> - Getting stronger and building up gradually prevents more injuries than anything else. In trials, strength training cut sports injuries to about a third, while stretching made no measurable difference.
> - Change one thing at a time: duration, ascent, pack weight, terrain or speed. Avoid sudden spikes in any of them, including a jump straight back to your pre-injury weights.
> - **Two pain rules, never mixed.** Your **injured arm** follows the arm pain rule: follow your physio's limit. If you weren't given one, keep pain at 2/10 or below during the exercise, back to your usual level by the next morning. Never train through pain that's increasing. **Everything else** (legs, back, your other arm) uses the traffic-light rule.
> - A fall is the injury that matters most for you, so a twisted ankle or a slip on wet leaves is also a risk to the arm.
> - Your other arm now does everything. Spread one-handed tasks across the day, and use the one-handed habits below for the commute, the changing room and the gym floor.

## Why lifters get hurt coming back from a layoff — and how you won't

Most overuse injuries happen when the load gets ahead of what a tissue can tolerate. Your heart and lungs adapt within weeks and muscles within weeks to months, but tendons and bone adapt more slowly **[Expert opinion]**. That gap is widest for an experienced lifter after time off. Muscle memory brings strength back quickly and your old working weights are still in your head, but weeks or months off have detrained your tendons and connective tissue too, and they rebuild on the slower clock **[Expert opinion]**. The classic comeback injury is loading to your old numbers in the first few weeks and ending up with sore knees, an angry Achilles or a tweaked back. The Re-entry Week and double progression let your loads find their level within a couple of sessions without outrunning your tendons.

Two findings anchor this chapter:
- In a meta-analysis of randomised trials, strength training cut sports injuries to under a third (risk ratio 0.32), and balance training cut them by about 45% (0.55). Stretching did nothing measurable (0.96) (Lauersen 2014) **[Strong evidence]**.
- In a survey of 1,295 Appalachian Trail hikers, those who hadn't trained beforehand had nearly three times the odds of a musculoskeletal injury (odds ratio 2.82) (Chrusch 2021) **[Moderate evidence]**.

The leg strength, core and balance work in your programme *is* your injury prevention. This chapter adds the rules that stop you outrunning it.

## Load management: the rules we use

1. **Change one thing at a time.** In any one week, add duration, ascent, pack weight, terrain difficulty or speed, but only one of them.
2. **Build gradually, but not timidly.** About 10% a week is a reasonable guide, not a law. In a trial of 532 novice runners, a gentler 13-week build-up didn't cut injuries compared with a standard 8-week plan (20.8% vs 20.3%) (Buist 2008) **[Moderate evidence]**. Avoiding sudden spikes seems to matter more, and a well-built base protects you (Gabbett 2016) **[Expert opinion]**.
3. **Follow the plan's built-in steps.** Long sessions grow gradually, and deload weeks halve the sets. After illness or a missed week, step back rather than catching up ([chapter 04](04-the-program.md)).
4. **Add pack weight late and slowly,** exactly as [the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule) says.
5. **Stop strength sets short of failure, and let your old numbers wait.** Stop at the reps in reserve the session shows (usually 2–3), lower under control, and never grind out a rep. Let double progression walk you back towards your old working weights rather than jumping straight to them.
6. **Check your readiness every morning.** The hub applies the same rule every day:

| Colour | Trigger | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| RED | Any arm red flag is ticked, or arm pain is 7/10 or more | Stop. No training today, including your physio exercises, until you've been advised. Contact your fracture clinic today, or NHS 111 / 999 as the red-flag guidance says |
| AMBER | Any of: resting HR at least 5 bpm above your 7-day average (once you have 5 readings since you last changed how you measure it), under 6 hours' sleep, arm pain of 4/10 or more, arm pain 2 or more points above its 7-day average, soreness of 7/10 or more, energy of 2/5 or less, or a day in a fasting period you've set in Settings | Easy day: halve the sets, Zone 2 only, no intervals, same loads. If it's rising arm pain, also call your fracture clinic today if it keeps rising or you're unsure |
| GREEN | None of the above | Train as planned |

## Two pain rules: which one applies

### Your injured arm: the arm pain rule

> [!SAFETY]
> **The arm pain rule (injured arm only, the same everywhere in the hub):** follow your physio's limit. If you weren't given one, keep pain at 2/10 or below during the exercise, back to your usual level by the next morning. Never train through pain that's increasing.
> - Until the gates allow it, the programme doesn't load the injured arm at all, so nothing outside your physio's programme should make it hurt.
> - If an exercise breaks the rule, stop it and tell your physio.
> - In the morning check-in, arm pain of 4/10 or more makes the day AMBER, and 7/10 or more makes it RED. A score 2 or more points above your 7-day average also makes it AMBER, because rising pain can be the red flag "pain getting worse": if it keeps rising, or you're unsure, call your fracture clinic today. Any arm red flag makes the day RED ([chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md)).

The traffic-light table below **never** applies to the injured arm, even after clearance. A healing bone, any plates and a scar don't follow the "some soreness is fine" logic that muscles and tendons do.

### Everything else: the traffic-light rule

Some discomfort is normal when you come back to training. The skill is knowing which kind to train through. Use this rule for your **legs, back and other arm** **[Expert opinion]**. It borrows from pain-monitored loading: people with Achilles tendinopathy who kept running and jumping within set pain limits did as well as those who stopped (Silbernagel 2007) **[Moderate evidence]**.

| Light | During or after activity | Next morning | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green | 0–3/10. Dull or achy, and eases as you warm up | Back to your normal within 24 hours | Carry on and progress as planned |
| Amber | 4–5/10, or creeping up from session to session | Still sore or stiff after 24 hours | Don't progress. For 1–2 weeks, cut that load by about a third to a half, or swap the exercise. If it isn't better after 2 weeks, see a physio |
| Red | 6/10 or more. Sharp or catching, or you're limping or moving differently | Worse than the day before, swollen, or aching at night or at rest | Stop that activity and train around it without pain. Get it checked if it isn't clearly settling within a few days, or sooner with swelling, giving way or a red-flag sign |

**Muscle soreness or pain to respect?** Delayed muscle soreness comes on 24–72 hours after a new exercise or your first sessions back, so expect more of it in your first fortnight. It's spread through the muscle, similar on both sides, and eases as you move. Pain to respect is sharp, pinpoint, or in a joint, tendon or bone, and it gets worse each session or shows up at night **[Expert opinion]**. Dark (cola-coloured) urine with severe muscle pain or swelling after training needs same-day medical advice (possible rhabdomyolysis).

## Falls: the injury that matters most for you

Until Full clearance, a fall onto the injured arm could undo months of healing, so fall-proofing beats every other prevention habit **[Expert opinion]**:
- **Build the legs and balance** that catch a stumble: step work, calves, hips, and balance next to a support every day ([chapter 07](07-mobility.md)).
- **Choose the surface:** dry, firm and level, in daylight. In winter that means no wet leaves, frost or towpath edges ([chapter 12](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md)).
- **Keep your uninjured hand free,** and use the rail on stairs and escalators.
- **Make every floor and machine transition one-handed and slow:** the uninjured-side method for getting to and from the floor ([chapter 07](07-mobility.md#getting-to-and-from-the-floor)), and on cardio machines, never step onto a moving belt or stairs ([chapter 10](10-engine-cardio.md)).
- **Treat wet changing-room tiles like ice** (see "Living one-handed" below).
- **If you start to slip,** bend your knees and sit down, with the injured arm tucked in.

Any fall onto the arm, even one that feels fine, means ticking the "fall, knock or jolt onto the arm" red flag and phoning the fracture clinic. The day turns RED: no training until you've spoken to them.

## Living one-handed: the commute, the changing room and the gym floor

Small habits remove most of the daily risk **[Expert opinion]**. What you carry, and how, follows [the carrying rule](18-arm-recovery.md#the-carrying-rule).

**Getting there:**
- **Carry your gym bag on the shoulder of your uninjured side,** 3 kg or less (shoes, towel, padlock, bottle), or use a waist pack. Put it on and take it off with your uninjured hand. Nothing goes on the injured side's shoulder: a strap there slides down the injured arm and tempts you to grab it with that hand.
- **Keep your uninjured hand free while you walk,** with your phone and bottle in a zipped pocket or the bag.
- **Hold rails with your uninjured hand.** On stairs, take the side where that hand reaches the rail, or use the lift. Where people stand on one side of an escalator (the right, in much of the UK), you can only reach that side's handrail, so if it's on your injured side, use the lift or the stairs instead.
- **On trains and buses, sit if you can.** If you're standing, hold a pole with your uninjured hand, keep your feet wide, and keep the injured arm across your body, away from doors and crowds. Some transport operators offer a free badge asking others to offer you a seat, such as TfL's "Please offer me a seat" badge in London. It's for people who struggle to stand on public transport, and you don't have to explain why (TfL).
- **Leave five minutes early.** Running for a train is a fall risk.

**In the changing room:**
- **Choose a locker at waist to chest height,** with a combination padlock or the gym's digital lock rather than fiddly keys.
- **Dress sitting down.** Put trousers, socks and shoes on while sitting on the bench, and never hop on one leg. The injured arm goes into sleeves first and comes out last. Loose tops, zip hoodies, elastic-waist joggers and slip-on or elastic-lace trainers all help.
- **Treat wet tiles like ice.** Wear grippy sliders in the showers, hold the grab rail with your uninjured hand, and dry your feet before you walk.
- **Pools, baths and saunas:** ask the clinic first. Any scab or gap in the wound means no soaking.

**On the gym floor:**
- **Ask a staff member once:** "I've got a healing arm fracture. Could you help me load the leg press and set its safety stops?" Staff load the plates until Good arm OK. After that your uninjured hand can load plates of 10 kg or less, one at a time and close to your body, and staff load anything heavier. The leg press uses a dead start, so you never touch the catches (see its exercise library entry).
- **Adjust seats and pins with your uninjured hand only,** and ask for help with any lever that needs two hands or a hard pull.
- **On the treadmill and stair climber,** clip the safety key on, rest your uninjured hand lightly on the rail with your injured arm across your stomach, and start and stop the machine while you're standing still on it. Never step onto a moving belt ([chapter 10](10-engine-cardio.md)).
- **Use a bottle with a flip cap,** and push doors with your uninjured hand or shoulder.
- **Train at quiet times if you can.** Fewer people means fewer bumps.

## Common overuse problems in hikers and lifters

The table combines NHS advice with common clinical practice **[Expert opinion]**.

| Problem | Where and when it hurts | Usual drivers | Prevention in your programme | See someone if… |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Front-of-knee (patellofemoral) pain | Around or behind the kneecap. Worse on stairs, downhill, squatting or after long sitting | Sudden jumps in downhill, stair or step-up volume | Hip and knee strength: `step-down-eccentric`, `spanish-squat-band`, `leg-extension`, `side-lying-hip-abduction`. Build descents gradually | It's no better after a few weeks (GP or physio). NHS 111 urgently if it locks, gives way, is badly swollen, or is hot and red with a high temperature |
| Outer-knee (iliotibial band) pain | Sharp pain on the outside of the knee, often 10–30 minutes into a walk, worse downhill | Sudden increases in distance or descent | Hip abductor work (`side-lying-hip-abduction`). Build downhill volume gradually | It isn't settling after 2 weeks of reduced load |
| Achilles tendon pain | Back of the heel or the tendon, stiff for the first steps in the morning | Rapid increases in stair-climber, incline or hill time | Calf strength (`calf-raise-single-bw`, `bent-knee-calf-raise`, and `calf-raise-leg-press` later), progressed by the traffic-light rule | A snap or pop, sudden severe pain, or you can't walk on tiptoe: NHS 111 urgently |
| Plantar heel pain | Under the heel or arch, worst on the first steps after rest | More walking on hard pavements, tight calves, poorly cushioned shoes | Cushioned, supportive footwear, calf strength, `calf-stretch-wall` | It's no better after 2 weeks of self-care, is severe, or comes with tingling or numbness (GP) |
| Shin pain | Front or inner shin, during or after walking | Starting after inactivity, hard pavements, sudden jumps in volume | `tibialis-raise-wall`, a gradual build-up | Pinpoint bone tenderness, pain at rest or at night, or it worsens despite rest. This could be a bone stress injury (GP) |
| Lower back pain | After lifting or a long walk with a pack | Load spikes, fatigue, bending first thing in the morning | Hinge strength, core endurance ([chapter 06](06-core-and-stability.md)), no loaded deep bending in the first hour after waking | See the urgent signs below |
| Elbow pain in your uninjured arm (tennis elbow) | Outside of the elbow, worse when gripping or twisting | Your uninjured arm doing every job, plus new grip training | Spread one-handed tasks, keep your bag light, build good-arm work gradually, don't max out your grip every day | It still hurts after 2 weeks of self-care (GP) |

The detail that matters:
- **Front-of-knee pain:** an international consensus recommends exercise therapy that combines hip and knee exercises, supports foot orthoses for short-term relief, and advises against knee and lower-back mobilisations and electrophysical treatments (Crossley 2016) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **Lower back:** there's no single "perfect" lifting shape. A 2020 review found low-quality evidence that a rounded back while lifting wasn't a risk factor for back pain (Saraceni 2020) **[Expert opinion]**. Avoiding forward bending early in the morning cut pain intensity by about a third in people with chronic back pain (Snook 1998) **[Moderate evidence]**, which matters if you train first thing.
- **NHS self-care** for knee, heel, shin and tendon pain is relative rest from whatever aggravates it, an ice pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes every 2–3 hours, supportive footwear and a gradual return. The NHS pages also mention ibuprofen, but **check with your fracture clinic before taking anti-inflammatories while your bone heals** ([chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md)).
- **Self-massage:** foam rolling usually means propping yourself up on your hands, so skip it until Full clearance. Use a massage ball against a wall, or a roller stick in your uninjured hand.
- **Your uninjured arm:** tennis elbow is linked to repeated gripping and twisting (NHS). Carry the gym bag light, take breaks from long one-handed tasks such as typing or phone use, and build grip work gradually once Good arm OK is set.

### Back pain — when to get urgent help

Call **999 or go to A&E** if back pain comes with:
- numbness around your genitals or bottom
- new problems controlling your bladder or bowels
- tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs
- chest pain
- pain that started after a serious accident

Contact your GP or NHS 111 urgently if you feel hot, cold, shivery or unwell, or the pain is severe and getting worse quickly (NHS).

## Blisters and ankle sprains

A systematic review of hiking injuries found that blisters (8–33% of injuries) and ankle sprains were the most common, and that the lower limb was injured most often (Braybrook 2023) **[Moderate evidence]**. A sprain matters twice over for you, because rolling an ankle is how people fall onto an arm.

- **Blisters:** prevention and hiker habits are in [chapter 11](11-hiking-training.md). In short: footwear that fits and is broken in, moisture-wicking socks, no popping, and a hydrocolloid dressing. Contact NHS 111 if a blister looks infected (NHS).
- **Ankle sprains (NHS):** for the first 2–3 days, protect, rest, ice, compress and raise the ankle, and avoid heat, alcohol and massage. Move gently once pain allows. Most sprains improve within about two weeks, but avoid intense activity for up to eight weeks. Call NHS 111 if you can't put weight on it, or 999 or A&E if you heard a crack, it looks misshapen, or the foot is numb, tingling, blue or cold.
- **Prevention:** balance training next to a support, calf and hip strength, footwear that fits, and good paths only until Full clearance.

## Warm-ups and footwear

The evidence that warming up prevents injury is suggestive but not conclusive: of five good-quality trials, three found fewer injuries and two found no difference (Fradkin 2006) **[Moderate evidence]**. A warm-up still makes the session go better, and dynamic movement gives small performance gains (Behm 2016) **[Moderate evidence]**. Your warm-ups and the morning "warm up before you bend" rule are in [chapter 07](07-mobility.md). Cool-downs are for comfort, and there's little evidence that they prevent injury **[Expert opinion]**.

**Footwear:** fit and comfort come first. Choose supportive shoes that fit, and break them in gradually (NHS). In the Appalachian Trail survey, stabilising footwear and poles were linked to fewer complaints (Chrusch 2021) **[Moderate evidence]**. In the gym, wear flat, stable trainers for leg work, because very soft running shoes are a poor choice for the leg press and step-ups. Elastic or lock laces save one-handed struggles **[Expert opinion]**. Walking shoes, boots and poles are covered in [chapter 12](12-hiking-skills-and-kit.md).

## When to see a physio, GP, NHS 111 or 999

| Situation | Who to contact | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Green or amber pain that's settling | Manage it yourself | Use the traffic-light rule, adapt the exercise, keep moving |
| Amber pain that isn't improving after 2 weeks, or keeps coming back | Physio | In some areas you can refer yourself to NHS community musculoskeletal (MSK) services (NHS) |
| Pain that stops normal activities, worsens despite self-care, or comes with tingling or numbness in the foot | GP or physio | Get it assessed rather than waiting it out |
| A hot, red, swollen joint with a high temperature, being unable to bear weight, or a snap or pop | NHS 111, urgently | Possible infection, rupture or fracture |
| You heard a crack, the limb looks misshapen, or it's numb, blue or cold | 999 or A&E | Don't drive yourself |
| **Any arm red flag, or arm pain of 7/10 or more** | Fracture clinic the same day. NHS 111 out of hours, or 999 if severe | The hub marks the day RED: no training until you've been advised ([chapter 18](18-arm-recovery.md)) |

## Sleep, stress and injury risk

- **Stress counts.** A meta-analysis of 48 studies found that, of the psychological factors studied, stress responses had the strongest link with injury (r = 0.27), and all seven prevention studies using psychological interventions reported fewer injuries (Ivarsson 2017) **[Moderate evidence]**.
- **The link with sleep is plausible but less clear.** In adolescent athletes, sleeping under 8 hours went with 1.7 times the injury rate (Milewski 2014). But a review of adult athletes concluded that poor sleep hasn't been shown to be an independent risk factor (Dobrosielski 2021) **[Moderate evidence]**. Sleep still drives recovery, mood and bone healing, so protect it ([chapter 17](17-sleep-and-recovery.md)).
- **The practical link:** short sleep, a stressful week or a raised resting HR all push the hub towards an AMBER day. On those days, the easy version is still training, not slacking.
